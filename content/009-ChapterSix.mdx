We are still in step two of the Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI) process. Now that we have an understanding of the core functional job ([Chapter 4](https://claude.ai/book/007-chapterFour)) and have created a job map ([Chapter 5](https://claude.ai/book/008-ChapterFive)), the next step is uncovering the needs (unmet needs) within the job steps. This is where the job map transforms from a structural framework into an innovation tool. Each step in the map becomes a container for specific, measurable things that customers are trying to achieve, and the gaps between what they want and what they currently get become your opportunities.

## What Is a Need (Outcome)?

<BookImage
  src="/images/book/chapter-06/outcomesUnderJobSteps.png"

  caption="Needs that belong to each job step illustration"
  width={600}
  height={324}

/>

In Outcome-Driven Innovation, the term "outcomes" is deliberately chosen over the more familiar "needs" to reflect important distinctions that make customer insights more actionable for innovation, Ulwick argues [31].

While needs often capture what customers think they lack or want in general terms, outcomes describe the specific, measurable end states customers are trying to achieve when executing a job. The change in terminology reflects a more measurement-focused approach to understanding customer value. Where a customer might express a need as "I need faster service," the corresponding outcome would be structured as "Minimize the time it takes to complete the transaction." Ulwick argues this specificity matters because it gives teams a clear metric (time), a specific object to optimize (transaction completion), and a direction for improvement (minimize) [31].

Unlike traditional needs statements, which can be vague, contaminated with solution ideas, or shifting based on current market context, properly structured need statements remain stable over time and solution-agnostic. They focus on the fundamental job the customer is trying to accomplish rather than their perceptions of what might help them.

### A Note on Terminology

Although from a theoretical perspective in Outcome-Driven Innovation we are supposed to call them "outcomes," for practical application in my day-to-day use of JTBD and ODI, **I will refer to them as "needs" for the rest of this book**.

The language of "needs" is much easier to communicate internally within organizations. Business stakeholders, product teams, and executives immediately understand what you mean when you talk about customer needs, whereas "outcomes" often requires explanation and can feel overly academic or consultant-heavy. While the theoretical distinction is important for understanding the framework's rigor, successful implementation depends on getting the whole team on board, and familiar language removes barriers to adoption.

What matters most is not the specific terminology we use, but that we maintain the underlying principles: specificity, measurability, and solution-agnostic thinking. Whether we call them needs or outcomes, we are still looking for the same thing: measurable success criteria that customers use to evaluate job execution. From this point forward, when I say "need" or "need statement," I mean what Ulwick would formally call a "desired outcome statement."

## Need Statement Syntax

<BookImage
  src="/images/book/chapter-06/desired-outcome-statement-example.png"

  width={600}

Need statements in this framework follow a structure that ensures they are measurable, actionable, and solution-agnostic. The syntax consists of four components:

**Direction of Improvement + Metric + Object of Control + Contextual Clarifier (optional)**

Let's break each one down.

**Direction of Improvement**

The direction of improvement indicates whether customers want to minimize or maximize something. In practice, Ulwick's convention is to use "minimize" for all need statements. The rationale is that "minimize" has a natural endpoint of zero, which makes it conceptually clean: you can always measure progress toward zero time, zero errors, zero waste.

I want to be transparent about this convention. You could argue that "minimize the likelihood of errors" and "maximize accuracy" are mathematical complements with equally clear endpoints (0% and 100% respectively). The reason the field standardizes on "minimize" is not that "maximize" is logically flawed, but that consistency makes analysis easier. When every need statement points in the same direction, you can compare and rank them without mental gymnastics about whether "high" is good or bad depending on the metric. Treat this as a practical convention for consistency, not a law of nature.

**Metric**

The metric specifies what is being measured. Common metrics include time, likelihood, number, amount, frequency, and effort. The metric should be something that can be measured or counted.

An important distinction here is between continuous metrics and binary metrics. Continuous metrics like time and amount can take any value along a range. Binary metrics like likelihood describe events that either happen or don't: the error either occurs or it doesn't, the data is either accurate or it isn't. "Likelihood" is the appropriate metric for these binary events ("minimize the likelihood of including outdated information"). For things that vary along a spectrum, use continuous metrics ("minimize the time it takes to locate supporting data"). This distinction matters because it affects how you measure satisfaction later in the quantification phase (Chapters 7 and 8). Keep it in mind as you write your need statements.

Avoid vague terms like "ease" or "convenience" as metrics. Instead, identify what makes something easy or convenient to measure. "Ease" is not measurable; "number of steps required" is.

**Object of Control**

The object of control identifies what element the customer wants to influence or optimize. This should describe an action, process, or condition that the customer is trying to accomplish as part of their job. It typically uses action verbs like "complete," "identify," "verify," "obtain," or "determine."

**Contextual Clarifier**

The contextual clarifier provides additional specificity about when, where, or under what circumstances this need applies. This helps distinguish between similar needs that might occur at different points in the job or under different conditions. For example, "minimize the time it takes to verify data accuracy" could apply to many job steps, but "minimize the time it takes to verify data accuracy before presenting to stakeholders" pins it to a specific moment in the job.

### Making Need Statements Work in Practice

The formal syntax we just covered is the foundation of the ODI methodology, and I will continue to use it throughout this book when discussing the remaining need types (consumption chain jobs, financial needs, emotional needs, and so on) to stay consistent with the framework. However it's important to point out... **I don't use the formal "minimize" syntax outside of methodology discussions**.

This is the same pragmatic choice we made earlier when we adopted "needs" over "outcomes." The formal terminology serves the methodology. Accessible language serves everyone else, and "everyone else" includes your **customers**, your **stakeholders**, and your **own team**.

The problem with the formal syntax is not just that business stakeholders find it alienating (though they do). The deeper issue is that it creates friction at every point where real humans have to interact with your need statements. When you put "minimize the likelihood of including outdated information" in a survey, respondents have to decode the structure before they can answer. When you present "minimize the time required to verify information credibility" in a product roadmap discussion, the room spends energy parsing the sentence instead of debating the priority. The language becomes a **barrier** between the insight and the action.

My recommendation is to use plain, natural language as your default for need statements in all contexts: interviews, surveys, stakeholder presentations, roadmap documents, and team discussions. The formal syntax is worth understanding because it teaches you the underlying discipline (every need should have a direction, a metric, an object, and context). But once you have internalized that discipline, you can express it in language that people actually talk in.

Here is what that looks like:

| **Formal ODI Syntax**                                                    | **Plain-Language Version (My Recommendation)**                 |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Minimize the time it takes to locate relevant supporting data            | Quickly find the right supporting data                         |
| Minimize the likelihood of including outdated information                | Confidently use current, accurate information                  |
| Minimize the time required to verify information credibility             | Quickly determine if a source is trustworthy                   |
| Minimize the likelihood of losing audience comprehension during delivery | Ensure the audience stays with you throughout the presentation |
| Minimize the likelihood that follow-up communications are misinterpreted | Clearly communicate next steps so nothing gets lost            |
| Minimize the number of steps required to confirm audience availability   | Easily confirm who can attend                                  |

Notice the pattern. "Minimize the time" becomes "quickly." "Minimize the likelihood" becomes "confidently," "ensure," or "reliably." "Minimize the number of steps" becomes "easily." The underlying metric and object of control are preserved, but the language shifts from measurement vocabulary to action vocabulary. The need is the same. The expression is just human.

A few guidelines for making this work well:

**Preserve the intent, not the structure**
The plain-language version does not need to follow the four-component syntax. What matters is that it captures the same need **without introducing solution language** or **losing specificity**. "Quickly find the right data" preserves the time metric and the object (finding data). "Make search better" does not. That is the line. You are simplifying the grammar, not diluting the meaning.

**This is especially important for surveys**
When you eventually quantify your needs (Chapters 7 and 8), your respondents will be real customers reading these statements and rating them. "Quickly find the right supporting data" is something a person can immediately evaluate from their own experience. "Minimize the time it takes to locate relevant supporting data for the specific topic being communicated" requires them to parse a 16-word noun phrase before they can even begin to form an opinion. Simpler language produces better survey data because respondents spend their cognitive effort on the rating, not on decoding the question.

**The discipline still matters, even without the syntax**
Dropping the "minimize" phrasing does not mean dropping the rigor. Every need statement you write should still have an implicit direction of improvement, an implicit metric, a clear object, and relevant context. "Quickly find the right data" has all four: the direction is "more quickly" (improve speed), the metric is time, the object is finding data, and "the right data" provides context. If you write a statement and cannot identify those four elements hiding inside it, the statement is too vague regardless of which syntax you used.

For the rest of this book, I will continue using the formal ODI syntax when introducing new need types to stay consistent with the methodology and to make the underlying structure visible. But when you apply these concepts to your own work, I encourage you to translate into plain language early and often. The goal is not to impress anyone with some framework and methodology. The goal is to produce insights that people can **understand**, **evaluate**, and **act on**.

## Uncovering Needs from Job Steps Through Interviews

This is where theory meets practice. The core skill of this chapter is learning to listen to what customers say about their struggles and translate their language into properly structured need statements. This translation is arguably the hardest skill in the entire ODI process, because customer language is messy, layered, and rarely maps one-to-one to a clean need statement.

Let's go back to our example from Chapter 5 with the core functional job "Communicate information clearly to an audience for understanding" and the job map we built. Below are excerpts from interviews with participants, organized by job step. For each exchange, I will show the interviewer's question, the customer's response, and then the translated need statement using the syntax we just defined.

### Job Step: Gather Evidence

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the step where you gather evidence or supporting data for your presentation. What makes this step challenging or time-consuming?

**Customer:** "Well, the biggest issue is that I can spend hours just trying to find the right information. I'll search through different databases, reports, and sources, and sometimes I'm not even sure if what I'm finding is actually relevant to what I need to communicate. It's frustrating because I know the information exists somewhere, but tracking it down takes forever."

_Translated need: Minimize the time it takes to locate relevant supporting data for the specific topic being communicated._

**Interviewer:** What about the quality of the information you find? What makes you confident or uncertain about using it?

**Customer:** "That's another headache. I'll find something that looks perfect, but then I realize it's from three years ago and the numbers have probably changed. Or I'll find conflicting information from different sources and I have to figure out which one is actually correct. I've been burned before by using outdated statistics in a presentation."

_Translated need: Minimize the likelihood of including outdated or inaccurate information in the final presentation._

**Interviewer:** How do you typically verify that your sources are credible and accurate?

**Customer:** "I usually try to cross-reference everything with at least two other sources, but that's so time-intensive. I'll spend almost as much time checking the information as I did finding it in the first place. Sometimes I wish there was a faster way to verify that what I'm looking at is legitimate and current."

_Translated need: Minimize the time required to verify information credibility across multiple sources._

### Job Step: Deliver Message (Execute)

**Interviewer:** When you are actually delivering the message, what is the hardest part of that moment?

**Customer:** "Honestly, the biggest challenge is reading the room while also staying on track with what I planned to say. I'll notice someone looks confused, and I have to make a split-second decision: do I stop and clarify, or do I push through because I'm running out of time? If I stop too often, I lose momentum and people disengage. If I don't stop, I might lose half the audience."

_Translated need: Minimize the likelihood of losing audience comprehension while maintaining presentation momentum._

**Interviewer:** What about when the audience is mixed, say some people are deeply technical and others are not?

**Customer:** "That's the worst. I'll be explaining something and the technical people are nodding along, clearly bored, while the non-technical people look lost. I basically have to find a way to keep both groups engaged at once, and I feel like I always end up disappointing one side."

_Translated need: Minimize the likelihood that content complexity is mismatched to audience expertise level._

### Job Step: Follow Up (Conclude)

**Interviewer:** After the communication event is over, what happens next?

**Customer:** "I always send a follow-up, but the problem is knowing whether anyone actually read it or acted on it. I'll send an email summarizing the key decisions and action items, and then two weeks later I find out that half the team didn't even open it, or they interpreted the action items differently than I intended. By that point it's too late to correct."

_Translated need: Minimize the likelihood that follow-up communications are misinterpreted or ignored by recipients._

**Interviewer:** How do you track whether the actions were completed?

**Customer:** "Honestly, it's pretty ad hoc. I might set a calendar reminder to check in with people, or I'll ask about it in our next team meeting. But I don't have a reliable way to know if the things we agreed on actually happened until something goes wrong."

_Translated need: Minimize the time it takes to confirm that agreed-upon actions have been completed._

### Handling Ambiguous or Tangled Customer Quotes

The examples above are relatively clean: one customer quote maps to one need statement. In practice, this is often not the case. Customers frequently express multiple needs in a single sentence, or their language blurs the line between a functional need and an emotional reaction.

Consider this quote from a different interviewee:

**Customer:** "I dread the preparation because I never know if I'm going to have enough time to make the slides look professional, and then I feel stupid if the data I present turns out to be wrong, and my boss notices."

There are at least three distinct things happening in this statement:

1. A functional need about preparation time: _Minimize the time required to create presentation materials that meet professional standards._
2. A functional need about data accuracy: _Minimize the likelihood that presented data contains errors._
3. An emotional/social need: _Avoid feeling incompetent when presenting inaccurate information to leadership._ (We will cover emotional needs later in this chapter.)

The temptation is to write one need statement that tries to capture everything. Resist that temptation. Separate the threads. Each distinct metric deserves its own statement. If a customer quote contains both a time problem and an accuracy problem, those are two different needs even though the customer experienced them as one frustration.

When you are unsure whether a quote represents one need or two, apply this test: could a product solve one of these problems without solving the other? If yes, they are separate needs. A tool could help you create professional slides faster (solving the time problem) without doing anything to verify data accuracy (the accuracy problem). These are different needs requiring potentially different solutions.

### Translating Customer Language into Proper Syntax

The translation process requires extracting the essence of what customers are trying to optimize from their descriptive language. Here are the patterns to listen for:

When customers say words like "faster," "quicker," "takes too long," or "I spend hours," they are expressing time-related needs. These translate to "minimize the time it takes to..." statements.

When customers mention "worried," "I've been burned," "I'm not sure if," or "what if," they are expressing likelihood-related needs. These translate to "minimize the likelihood of..." statements.

When customers describe effortful activities with phrases like "I have to manually," "it takes so many steps," or "I have to keep checking," they are expressing effort or frequency-related needs. These translate to "minimize the number of..." or "minimize the effort required to..." statements.

In all cases, strip out solution references and context-specific details while preserving the core metric. If a customer says "I hate having to check three different systems to verify the data is current," focus on the underlying need to reduce verification time rather than the systems mentioned. The fact that there are three systems is a detail about their current workaround, not part of the need itself.

### Interview Questions for Uncovering Need Statements

Below are question sets organized by purpose. Use them as a toolkit, not a script. You will not ask all of these in every interview. Choose the questions that are most relevant to the job steps you are investigating.

#### Broad Exploratory Questions

Start with broad questions about each job step to uncover pain points and friction:

- What makes [step] challenging or frustrating?
- What are the biggest obstacles to getting [step] done efficiently?
- What makes [step] time-consuming or slow?
- What causes [step] to go off track or become problematic?
- What parts of [step] require the most effort or attention?
- What makes [step] unpredictable or inconsistent?

#### Measurement and Comparison Questions

These reveal the metrics customers actually care about:

- How do you measure success when trying to [step]?
- What makes one approach better than another for [step]?
- How do you know when [step] is done well versus poorly?
- What would "perfect" look like for [step]?
- How do you currently track progress during [step]?

#### Need-Specific Probes

These directly target performance criteria:

- What needs to be minimized during [step]? (Identifies things to reduce: time, errors, cost, risk)
- What must be ensured or guaranteed during [step]? (Reveals critical success factors)
- When [step] goes perfectly, what results do you see?
- What would make [step] feel effortless or automatic?

#### Context and Constraint Questions

These help understand situational factors that influence needs:

- What information do you need to feel confident during [step]?
- What resources or tools are essential for [step] to work well?
- What external factors can make [step] more difficult?
- When does [step] need to happen faster or slower?
- Who else is affected when [step] doesn't go well?

## Beyond the Core Job: A Map of the Full Landscape

Now that we have an understanding of how to uncover the functional needs within the core job, let's expand our view. A customer's experience doesn't end with the core job. They exist within a network of needs and challenges that span multiple dimensions.

In the following sections, we will explore several types of these interconnected needs. I have ordered them roughly by their proximity to the core job:

1. **Consumption Chain Jobs** are closest to the product itself, covering everything customers must do to acquire, use, maintain, and dispose of your solution.
2. **Financial Needs** capture the economic dimension of getting the job done.
3. **Emotional and Social Needs** capture how customers want to feel and be perceived.
4. **Related Jobs** represent the furthest ring: genuinely separate functional objectives that happen before, after, or alongside the core job and point toward adjacent market opportunities.
5. **Complexity Factors** cut across all of the above, explaining why the same job is harder for some customers than others.

Before we dive in, a word about volume. If you follow all the guidance in this chapter thoroughly, you could generate 50 or more functional need statements from the job map, plus 10 to 20 emotional and social needs, plus 20 to 30 consumption chain jobs, plus 15 to 20 financial needs. That is potentially 100+ statements before you even get to quantification. As I warned back in Chapter 2, 100+ statements are overkill for most teams.

Here is how I think about managing this: the functional need statements from the core job map are non-negotiable. They are the backbone of your research and where the most valuable innovation insights live. Everything else (emotional needs, consumption chain jobs, financial needs, related jobs) should be calibrated to your team's capacity and strategic priorities. A startup validating product-market fit might focus exclusively on functional needs and skip consumption chain jobs entirely. An enterprise team planning a multi-year platform strategy might need the full inventory. Use the sections below as a menu, not a mandate. Invest depth where it will drive your most important decisions, and skim or skip where it won't.

## Consumption Chain Jobs

  src="/images/book/chapter-06/consumptionChainJobs.png"

Think of consumption chain jobs as the "work" a customer has to do to make a product or service effective for their core job. Unlike everything else we have discussed so far, consumption chain jobs are deliberately **not** solution-agnostic. They are tied to a specific solution (e.g., "monitor blood glucose levels using a particular meter"). They cover every interaction a customer has with a product or service from the moment they consider it to the moment they discard it.

This might seem like a contradiction. The entire ODI framework is built on solution agnosticism, so why are we suddenly introducing solution-specific jobs? The answer is that consumption chain jobs serve a different purpose than functional need statements.

Functional needs help you innovate the core value proposition like what the product should do. Consumption chain jobs help you improve the experience around the product. For example how easy it is to buy, set up, use, maintain, and retire. Both matter, but they answer different questions. Think of functional needs as innovating the "what" and consumption chain jobs as optimizing the "how."

The syntax for a consumption chain job is: **Verb + Object of the Verb (product/service) + Contextual Clarifier (optional)**

Examples:

- Purchase medical supplies for the BG meter when traveling abroad.
- Maintain the car's engine according to the manufacturer's schedule.
- Clean the washing machine's drum after heavy use.

### Why Consumption Chain Jobs Matter

These jobs offer insights into potential pain points and opportunities for differentiation within the product lifecycle. By understanding the entire journey a customer takes with your product, you can:

**Identify hidden friction**

Customers often tolerate inefficiencies or frustrations in their consumption chain without realizing there could be a better way. A product might perfectly fulfill its core function, but if it is difficult to set up, clean, or repair, the overall customer experience suffers. Uncovering these allows you to design solutions that simplify, streamline, or eliminate unnecessary tasks.

**Enhance loyalty and retention**

Many churn events happen not because the product failed at its core job, but because the consumption chain became too burdensome. A software tool that works perfectly but requires painful annual license renewal is losing customers to a consumption chain problem, not a functional one.

**Create new service offerings**

Consumption chain jobs can often be spun off into standalone services. A company selling complex machinery might offer installation, maintenance, or repair services. A SaaS company might offer onboarding consulting or data migration support. These represent revenue opportunities that emerge directly from understanding what customers struggle with around the product.

### Consumption Chain Examples Across Industries

**Automotive Industry (Product: Car)**

- Purchase the car with financing options.
- Install a child safety seat in the vehicle.
- Learn to use the car's infotainment system.
- Clean the car's interior after a road trip.
- Monitor the car's tire pressure regularly.
- Maintain the car's engine according to the manufacturer's schedule.
- Repair the car's body after a minor collision.
- Dispose of the old car when upgrading to a new model.

**Software Industry (Service: Project Management Software)**

- Select the project management software based on team size.
- Confirm the subscription plan for annual billing.
- Initiate the service for new team members.
- Receive technical support when encountering an error.
- Pay for the service monthly via credit card.
- Monitor the delivery of new features via email updates.
- Modify the service settings to fit project requirements.
- Resolve integration problems with existing tools.
- Comply with data privacy requirements for sensitive project information.
- Conclude the service at the end of a project cycle.

### Things to Watch Out For

**Specificity matters**
Avoid vague statements. "Maintain the car" is a starting point, but "Maintain the car's oil level before long trips" is more actionable. The more specific the consumption chain job, the clearer the problem and the potential solution.

**Focus on the task, not your brand**
While consumption chain jobs are product-related, the goal is still to understand the task the customer is trying to accomplish, not how they interact with your specific product. "Purchase medical supplies" is better than "Buy our specific brand of medical supplies."

**Frame from the customer's perspective**
Always write consumption chain jobs using the customer's language and from their point of view, not from your internal operations perspective. "Set up the software" not "Onboard the user."

**Aim for comprehensiveness**
A thorough exercise typically uncovers 20 to 30 distinct consumption chain jobs. Companies often overlook routine steps because they feel obvious, but those routine steps are frequently where the most persistent friction lives.

**Distinguish products from services**
While many generic consumption chain jobs overlap, some steps are unique to physical products (install, transport, store, dispose) and others are unique to services (subscribe, comply with requirements, conclude). Make sure your list reflects the actual nature of your offering.

### Questions to Uncover Consumption Chain Jobs

- What products or services are you using when trying to get your core job done?
- What tasks do you have to complete when acquiring the product or service (ordering, purchasing, installing)?
- What tasks do you have to accomplish during ongoing use of the product or service (maintaining, updating, cleaning, configuring)?
- What tasks arise when you stop using a product or service (disposing, canceling, migrating data, returning)?
- What workarounds have you developed for any part of the product lifecycle?

## Financial Needs

When a customer decides to buy a product or service, they aren't just thinking about the task they need to accomplish. They are also making an economic decision. Financial needs are the economic criteria customers want to optimize when getting their core job done. They represent the customer's desire for efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and value.

### Financial Need Syntax and Structure

Financial needs follow the same foundational syntax as functional needs:

**Direction of Improvement + Metric + Object of Control + Contextual Clarifier (optional)**

As with functional needs, use "minimize" consistently as the direction of improvement for the same reasons: it creates a uniform direction that makes analysis and comparison straightforward.

**Metric**

The metric in financial needs specifies the economic or financial unit being measured. Common financial metrics include:

- **Costs of**: Direct monetary expenses (purchase costs, operating costs, maintenance costs).
- **Risk of**: Financial exposure to loss or variance (risk of going over budget, risk of penalties, risk of asset depreciation).
- **Amount of**: Quantities that have direct financial implications (amount of waste, amount of rework, amount of inventory).
- **Frequency of**: How often costly events occur (frequency of paid repairs, frequency of purchasing replacement parts).
- **Downtime of**: Time-based losses with economic impact (downtime of revenue-generating equipment).
- **Time to**: Duration metrics with cost implications (time to payback, time to break-even).
- **Likelihood of**: Reserved for binary financial events (likelihood of incurring a late fee, likelihood of triggering a tax audit). As we discussed in the functional needs syntax section, "likelihood" is the appropriate metric for events that either happen or don't, as opposed to continuous metrics like time or cost that vary along a range.

**Object of Control**

The object of control identifies the source or driver of costs and economic inefficiencies. This pinpoints what element causes financial impact:

- Purchase-related: costs of acquisition, financing, procurement processes.
- Operational: costs of training, implementation, integration, maintenance.
- Lifecycle: costs of storing, transporting, installing, upgrading, disposing.
- Financial exposure: risk of cost overruns, risk of non-compliance penalties, risk of budget variance.
- Process inefficiencies: amount of rework, waste generation, manual intervention.
- Resource utilization: downtime of personnel, underutilization of assets.

**Contextual Clarifier**

The contextual clarifier specifies the circumstances under which these financial impacts occur: when (during peak seasons, at project start-up), where (in particular departments or environments), for what purpose (when scaling operations, for compliance requirements), or under what conditions (with limited resources, during high-demand periods).

### Financial Need Examples Across Industries

Notice how the focus stays on the cost or risk of the outcome, rather than the functional error itself.

**B2B Software (CRM Platform)**

- Minimize the cost of onboarding a new salesperson.
- Minimize the cost of rework caused by data entry errors.
- Minimize the cost of generating a quarterly sales report.

**Healthcare (Surgical Device)**

- Minimize the cost of disposables required for each procedure.
- Minimize the financial loss associated with patient readmissions.
- Minimize the cost of sterilization downtime between procedures.

**Consumer Goods (Washing Machine)**

- Minimize the cost of electricity and water consumed per laundry cycle.
- Minimize the frequency of paid repairs needed over the product's lifespan.
- Minimize the amount of detergent wasted due to inefficient dispensing.

### Things to Watch Out For

**Go beyond price.** The most powerful financial needs are often related to the costs of usage, maintenance, and the consequences of inefficiency, not just the initial purchase price. Teams that focus only on "minimize the cost of the product" miss the larger economic picture.

**Identify the purchase decision maker.** The person using the product (the job executor) may not be the one making the purchase decision. A doctor might use a medical device, but a hospital administrator approves the purchase based on a different set of financial criteria. This was briefly addressed in Chapter 3 when we discussed job executors, but it has particular relevance here. When uncovering financial needs, you may need to interview both the user and the buyer, because their financial priorities often differ significantly.

**Aim for 15 to 20 statements.** A thorough investigation should yield a complete set of financial need statements covering the full economic picture of getting the job done. If you are well under this range, you may be overlooking lifecycle costs, opportunity costs, or risk-related financial needs.

### Interview Questions for Uncovering Financial Needs

**Cost Discovery Questions:**

- What are the hidden or unexpected costs that emerge when getting this job done?
- Which aspects of getting this job done consume the most budget or resources?
- What costs do you incur when things go wrong or need to be redone?
- What are the opportunity costs of the time and resources spent on this job?

**Risk and Loss Questions:**

- What financial risks do you face if this job isn't done properly or on time?
- What does it cost you when delays occur in getting this job done?
- How much do errors or mistakes in this job typically cost to fix?
- What revenue or savings opportunities do you miss when this job takes too long?

**Resource and Investment Questions:**

- What is the total cost of ownership for your current approach to this job?
- How much do you invest in training people to get this job done effectively?
- What does it cost to scale up when demand for this job increases?
- How much do you spend on tools, systems, or infrastructure to support this job?

**Efficiency and Waste Questions:**

- Where do you see the most waste or inefficiency in getting this job done?
- What redundant activities or processes add unnecessary costs?
- How much do compliance or regulatory requirements add to the cost?
- What would eliminating bottlenecks in this process be worth to you?

## Emotional and Social Needs

<BookImage
  src="/images/book/chapter-06/socialemotionalneeds.png"
  alt="Personal Emotional and Social Needs"
  caption="Personal Emotional and Social Needs"
  width={600}
  height={741}
  standalone={true}
/>

Teams frequently overlook emotional needs, even though they drive many purchase decisions. While functional needs describe what customers want to accomplish, emotional needs reveal how customers want to feel and be perceived throughout their journey. These needs operate on two distinct levels. Personal emotional needs that focus on internal feelings and experiences, and social needs that center on external perceptions and social positioning.

While the core job provides the functional value of your product, emotional and social needs create the connection. A product that only solves the functional job is a tool. One that also addresses emotional needs becomes something people prefer. Customers may need your product because of what it does, but they will prefer your product because of how it makes them feel.

### Emotional Needs

Emotional needs define the internal emotional states customers seek to achieve or avoid when getting their job done. These needs are tied to basic human drives for control, security, pleasure, esteem, freedom, belonging, and calm. A customer purchasing financial planning software, for instance, may have the personal emotional need to "feel confident when managing their financial future" or "avoid feeling anxious about retirement planning."

These emotional needs often manifest as underlying motivations that drive functional behavior. The customer who meticulously researches every product feature before making a purchase may be driven by the emotional need to feel secure and avoid regret. Understanding personal emotional needs helps organizations recognize that customers aren't just buying products or services. They are seeking emotional states that enhance their sense of well-being, competence, or fulfillment.

### Social Needs

Social needs focus on how customers want to be perceived by others in their social and professional circles. These needs reflect our nature as social beings who care about reputation, status, belonging, and identity. They often cluster around themes of power and confidence, security and pride, impact and status, belonging and approval, or individuality and uniqueness.

A professional using collaboration software may want to "be perceived as an organized team leader by colleagues" or "avoid being perceived as technologically incompetent by peers." Social needs can be just as powerful as functional requirements in driving purchase decisions and product adoption. They help explain why customers sometimes choose solutions that may be functionally adequate but excel at supporting their desired social positioning.

### Emotional and Social Need Syntax

Emotional and social needs require a different verb structure than functional and financial needs. Functional and financial needs are about optimizing metrics (minimize time, minimize cost). Emotional needs are about achieving or avoiding internal states. Social needs are about shaping external perceptions. This means we use a different set of lead verbs:

- **"Feel"** for positive personal emotions you want to achieve.
- **"Avoid feeling"** for negative personal emotions you want to prevent.
- **"Be perceived as"** for positive social perceptions you want to create.
- **"Avoid being perceived as"** for negative social perceptions you want to prevent.

After the verb comes the emotional state or persona. This should be specific and meaningful rather than generic. Instead of "good" or "bad," use precise descriptors like "confident," "secure," "respected," "competent," or "innovative."

Finally, add a contextual clarifier that specifies when, where, or in what situation this emotional need applies.

Properly structured examples:

- Feel confident when presenting ideas to senior leadership.
- Avoid feeling overwhelmed when managing multiple projects simultaneously.
- Be perceived as a tech-savvy professional by colleagues.
- Avoid being perceived as irresponsible by family members when making financial decisions.

### When and How to Capture Emotional and Social Needs

A common question is whether emotional needs get captured during the same interviews where you are uncovering functional needs, or whether they require separate research.

In my experience, the most effective approach is to weave emotional and social questions into your functional need interviews rather than conducting them separately. After you have explored a job step and uncovered the functional needs, you can naturally pivot to the emotional dimension: "When that step goes wrong, how does that make you feel?" or "When you nail this step, how do you want your team to see you?" This approach works because the customer is already grounded in the specific context of the job step, which produces more specific and authentic emotional responses than abstract questions asked in isolation.

However, emotional needs do not always map neatly to individual job steps. Some emotional needs span the entire job ("feel in control throughout the process") while others attach to specific moments ("avoid feeling embarrassed during the presentation"). Do not force every emotional need into a specific job step. Capture them as they emerge and organize them later. Some will attach to steps; others will float above the map as overarching emotional themes.

### Questions for Identifying Emotional and Social Needs

**Personal Emotional Needs:**

- How do you want to feel when doing this job?
- What feelings do you want to avoid when trying to accomplish this task?
- If you had the ideal solution for this situation, how would that make you feel?
- What gives you confidence when working on [step]?
- What makes you feel stressed or anxious about [step]?
- When [step] goes well, what positive feelings do you experience?
- What would make you feel more in control during [step]?

**Social Needs:**

- When trying to accomplish this job, how do you want to be perceived by others (peers, clients, family, friends)?
- How do you want to avoid being perceived by people who matter to you?
- What social situations make [step] more stressful or uncomfortable?
- How important is it that others see you as competent at [step]?
- What would prevent you from feeling judged or criticized when doing [step]?

### Things to Watch Out For

**Aim for completeness without redundancy**
Aim for 10 to 20 distinct emotional and social needs per core job. This range comes from practical experience: fewer than 10 usually means you have not probed deeply enough, and more than 20 typically means you are capturing variations of the same underlying emotion with slightly different wording. If you find yourself writing "feel confident about data accuracy," "feel confident about data completeness," and "feel confident about data timeliness," consider whether these are truly distinct or whether they collapse into one broader need about data confidence.

**Keep emotional needs solution-agnostic**
Like functional needs, emotional needs should not reference specific technologies, products, or services. Focus on the underlying emotional state desired, not the mechanism for achieving it.

**Don't blend functional and emotional needs**
Each need statement should be purely one type or the other. "Feel confident that the data is accurate" is an emotional need. "Minimize the likelihood that data contains errors" is a functional need. They are related but distinct. The functional need describes a measurable outcome; the emotional need describes a desired internal state. Capture both, but keep them separate.

**Remember that emotional needs enable segmentation**
Different customer segments may share similar functional requirements while having distinctly different emotional needs. A surgeon and a general practitioner may both need to "minimize the time to diagnose a condition," but their emotional needs around confidence, professional reputation, and patient trust may differ substantially. These emotional variations can become powerful segmentation variables later in the process.

## Related Jobs

Related jobs represent functional tasks that customers must accomplish alongside their core job. Their practical value lies primarily in identifying market expansion opportunities rather than comprehensively mapping every customer activity.

### The Trap: Confusing Job Steps with Related Jobs

The primary pitfall with related jobs is the tendency to fragment what should be understood as a unified customer journey. Teams often mistake integral steps of the core job for separate related jobs. This leads to disjointed product roadmaps and a failure to see the whole picture.

When analyzing the job "purchase products online," activities like verifying order details or tracking shipment status are not separate jobs. They are essential execution steps of the purchasing process. A thorough job map ([Chapter 5](https://claude.ai/book/008-ChapterFive)) naturally captures these activities and their associated needs.

### The Independence Test

To determine whether something is a step or a related job, apply the Independence Test:

- **A Job Step** must happen to complete the core job successfully. If you remove it, the core job fails.
- **A Related Job** can be performed independently, at a different time, or by a completely different vendor, without breaking the core job.

### A Note on Ambiguity Between Related Jobs and Higher-Level Core Jobs

Readers may notice a tension between related jobs and the "flight levels" concept from Chapter 4. In the table above, "Discovering new artists" is listed as a related job to "Listen to music." But in Chapter 5's Spotify example, we elevated the core job to "Discover audio content for personal enjoyment and inspiration," which arguably encompasses both listening and discovery as steps within a single higher-level job.

This is not a contradiction. It is a matter of perspective. At the flight level of "Listen to music," discovery is a related job. At the higher flight level of "Discover audio content," discovery becomes central to the core job and listening becomes one of its steps. Which framing is "correct" depends on the strategic question you are trying to answer. If you are optimizing a music player, discovery is a related job that points to expansion opportunities. If you are building an audio content platform, discovery is the core job and listening is one step within it.

When you encounter these ambiguities, ask yourself: at which flight level is my team operating? That determines what counts as core and what counts as adjacent.

### Using Related Jobs Strategically

Since a well-constructed job map already captures the complete customer journey for the core job, you do not need to stress over creating exhaustive inventories of related jobs. Instead, use related jobs as a strategic growth tool.

In my experience, teams rarely need to map related jobs during the initial core product definition. Return to related jobs when you are looking for growth strategies. Ask: "Once our customer gets the core job done perfectly, what are they trying to accomplish next?" That question is where your adjacent market opportunities live.

For example, a music streaming service that masters the core job of "listening to music" might look at the related job of "discovering new artists." This leads to social recommendation features or concert integration, features that expand the product's scope beyond simple streaming. That is genuine growth, not feature tweaking.

## Complexity Factors

When customers attempt to get a job done, their experiences vary based on their circumstances. Some customers complete the job effortlessly, while others encounter obstacles and frustration along the way. These varying levels of difficulty stem from what ODI calls complexity factors. These are the characteristics of the job executor, their environment, or their situation that create barriers to successful job completion.

### Why Complexity Factors Matter

Understanding complexity factors serves three interconnected purposes that become increasingly important as you move through the ODI process.

First, they highlight the root causes behind customer struggle, enabling teams to design solutions that address real barriers to job completion rather than just surface-level symptoms.

Second, they provide the foundation for effective needs-based segmentation (Chapter 9). If your research reveals that "time pressure" is a situational complexity factor, your quantitative survey might show that customers who frequently execute the job under tight deadlines prioritize speed-related needs far more than customers with flexible timelines. These patterns become the raw material for identifying distinct segments.

Third, complexity factors serve as profiling and screening variables for your segments. After statistical analysis identifies needs-based segments, complexity factors help explain why those segments have different priorities. Some complexity factors also become survey questions that help you identify which segment a customer belongs to for targeting purposes. As you identify complexity factors in this chapter, document the observable characteristic, how it appears to affect job difficulty, and which needs it impacts most. This documentation will become valuable when you design your quantitative survey in Chapter 7 and analyze segments in Chapter 9.

### Pre-Research Assessment: Filtering for Systemic Barriers

Before diving into the three categories of complexity factors, there is a strategic step worth taking. Before conducting extensive customer interviews or quantitative research, teams should create a hypothesized job map of their core job and analyze each step for systemic barriers that might constrain your ability to innovate, regardless of what customer needs you uncover.

This pre-research assessment involves identifying job steps where solutions already exist but operate in heavily regulated environments, where your product could fulfill the job step effectively but systemic factors prevent market entry or adoption, or where regulatory requirements, industry standards, or entrenched infrastructure create persistent barriers across all job executions.

**Examples:**

In healthcare, consider the job "Monitor patient health remotely." A team might identify the step "Transmit patient data" and realize that while their IoT device technically solves this need, HIPAA compliance, FDA device approval requirements, and hospital IT integration standards create systemic barriers that could take years and significant capital to navigate.

In financial services, the job "Transfer money internationally" includes the step "Verify transaction compliance." A fintech startup might build superior technology for this, but anti-money laundering regulations, banking partnership requirements, and international regulatory frameworks create systemic complexity that established players are better positioned to handle.

Once teams complete this analysis, they can have an informed strategic discussion about whether to proceed knowing the systemic challenges, pivot to adjacent job areas, or reframe the core job to focus on areas where systemic factors are less constraining. This prevents teams from discovering fundamental market barriers only after investing heavily in customer research.

### Three Categories of Complexity Factors

Complexity factors fall into three distinct categories, each creating different types of barriers to job completion and requiring different solution approaches.

**Person-Related Complexity Factors** encompass the characteristics of both the primary job executor and other stakeholders involved in the process. For the main executor, these include their skills, knowledge, experience level, attitudes, physical capabilities, and how frequently they perform the job. Additionally, the characteristics of other people involved (clients, patients, assisting personnel) can make the job harder. A novice executor working with experienced stakeholders faces different challenges than an expert working with uninformed participants.

**Situational Context Complexity Factors** represent the immediate, variable circumstances surrounding a specific job execution instance. These include the physical environment, available space, weather conditions, social dynamics, time pressures, stress levels, and whether the executor is multitasking or traveling. In healthcare, this might mean the difference between a routine visit and an emergency situation, specific patient conditions, or which staff members are available that day. Situational factors create variable complexity because they change from one job execution to another, making the same job easy in some circumstances and difficult in others.

**Systemic Contextual Factors** represent the persistent structural realities that consistently shape how jobs must be executed across all instances. These include regulatory frameworks, industry standards, organizational policies, technological infrastructure, economic systems, and cultural norms. In healthcare, examples include insurance approval processes, HIPAA compliance requirements, and hospital IT systems that affect every job execution. Unlike situational factors, systemic factors create persistent complexity that remains constant across job executions, though different executors may be better equipped to navigate these structural barriers based on their resources, experience, or position within the system.

The key distinction between situational and systemic factors is persistence and variability. Systemic factors remain constant across job executions for all users in that domain, while situational factors change from instance to instance. Systemic complexity often requires ecosystem-level solutions, partnerships, or tools that help users navigate persistent barriers. Situational complexity can often be addressed through more tactical solutions like improved interfaces, flexible workflows, or adaptive features.

### Identifying Complexity Factors

Different research methods reveal different types of complexity, and teams need systematic approaches to uncover factors that may not be immediately obvious.

**Interview Techniques for Uncovering Complexity**

Customer interviews excel at revealing person-related and situational complexity factors, but teams must probe beyond surface-level responses. When customers describe job execution, they often normalize their struggles or assume certain difficulties are universal. Effective complexity factor discovery requires specific questioning techniques.

Start with broad process mapping, then drill into moments of friction. Use contrast questions to surface hidden complexity:

- "How was that different from the time before?" reveals situational variability.
- "What would make this step much easier or much harder?" uncovers potential complexity drivers.
- "Walk me through your worst experience doing this" exposes multiple complexity factors intersecting.

Pay attention to workarounds and compensating behaviors. When customers mention "I usually..." or "I make sure to..." they are often revealing complexity factors they have learned to navigate. A project manager saying "I always schedule an extra 30 minutes for client calls" might be compensating for systemic factors like unreliable video conferencing or situational factors like variable client preparation levels.

As you uncover complexity factors, document them in a format that can translate into quantitative screening or segmentation questions. For each factor, note the observable or self-reported characteristic, how it appears to affect job execution difficulty, and which needs seem most impacted.

**Secondary Research for Systemic Complexity**

While interviews reveal personal and situational complexity, systemic factors often require broader industry research. Look for indicators in regulatory databases and compliance guides, industry reports discussing persistent pain points across organizations, professional association publications about navigating standards, and competitive analysis showing where established players maintain advantages despite inferior technology.

### Things to Watch Out For

**Customers aren't always aware**
People often cannot articulate why something is difficult; they just know that it is. You have to act like a detective, observing their situation and connecting the dots across interviews.

**Complexity factors are not demographics**
A demographic like "senior citizen" is not a complexity factor. The underlying factor might be "declining eyesight" or "limited mobility." Focus on the functional challenge, not the demographic label. This distinction becomes vital in Chapter 9. Needs-based segments are defined by what people are trying to accomplish, not who they are demographically. Complexity factors help you understand the functional reality behind surface-level descriptors.

**Build and test hypotheses**
You will rarely be told a complexity factor directly. Listen for clues, form a hypothesis (e.g., "I think job execution is harder for people with an irregular income"), and then look for evidence to support or refute it in subsequent interviews.

**Watch for the expertise trap**
Experienced job executors often provide misleading complexity factor data because they have internalized workarounds. An experienced professional saying "it's straightforward once you know the system" may be masking complexity factors that affect newer executors or different contexts. Always balance expert interviews with novice perspectives.

## What Comes Next: From Inventory to Quantification

At this point, you have built a comprehensive inventory of customer needs. You have functional needs from each job step, emotional and social needs, financial considerations, complexity factors, and consumption chain jobs. This is the qualitative foundation: a solution-agnostic picture of everything customers are trying to accomplish and the barriers standing in their way.

But a list of 50, 80, or even 125 needs doesn't tell you where to focus. Not all needs are created equal. Some represent real opportunities for differentiation; others are table stakes that every competitor already addresses well. The question shifts from "What do customers need?" to "Which needs matter most, and which are currently underserved?"

This is where quantification comes in. The next chapter examines how to measure and prioritize this inventory so you can make confident decisions about where to invest. We will start with the traditional ODI approach: a dual importance-and-satisfaction survey that has become the standard in the field. But we will also examine its limitations. The surveys are long. The scoring algorithm has statistical assumptions worth questioning. And the practical constraints often make it difficult to execute well.

Understanding both the promise and the problems with traditional quantification will prepare you to evaluate the alternatives in Chapter 8, which maintain the goal of clear prioritization while addressing the methodological shortcomings that can lead teams astray.

## Chapter 6 Summary

- **Needs are measurable success criteria.** We defined need statements as the specific, measurable end states customers are trying to achieve. For practical communication with internal teams, it is effective to use the familiar term "customer needs" while retaining the ODI syntax of Direction of Improvement + Metric + Object of Control + Contextual Clarifier.
- **The "minimize" convention is about consistency, not logic.** The field standardizes on "minimize" as the direction of improvement because it creates a uniform direction for analysis, not because "maximize" is inherently flawed. Emotional needs require a different verb structure ("Feel" / "Avoid feeling" / "Be perceived as" / "Avoid being perceived as") because they describe states to achieve rather than metrics to optimize.
- **Translating customer language is the core skill.** The central challenge of this chapter is learning to listen to customer interviews and translate descriptions of challenges, frustrations, and struggles into properly structured need statements. When a single customer quote contains multiple needs, separate them. Apply the test: could a product solve one without solving the other?
  - Here is a [link](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1CRq_nkX3K66oQ-2zHYYOLRvbR_wCL7LeV2mGMQs59cg/edit?usp=sharing) to an interview guide with all the key questions to ask during a JTBD and ODI interview.
- **Innovation exists beyond the core job.** To create holistic solutions, look beyond the primary task and uncover interconnected needs:
  - **Consumption Chain Jobs:** The work customers must do to purchase, set up, use, maintain, and dispose of a product or service. Unlike other need types, these are deliberately solution-specific.
  - **Financial Needs:** The economic efficiencies customers want to achieve, such as minimizing costs, waste, or the risk of financial loss.
  - **Emotional and Social Needs:** How customers want to feel (personal) and be perceived (social) when performing the job. Capture these during functional need interviews by pivoting to emotional questions after exploring each job step.
  - **Related Jobs:** Genuinely separate functional objectives that happen alongside the core job and point toward adjacent markets. Use the Independence Test to distinguish these from job steps.
- **Complexity factors reveal the "why" behind difficulty.** Three types of barriers affect job execution:
  - **Person-Related:** The executor's skills, experience, or physical capabilities.
  - **Situational Context:** The variable environment, time pressures, or location.
  - **Systemic Context:** The persistent rules, regulations, or infrastructure that affect everyone.
- **Complexity factors enable segmentation.** The factors you document here will be used in Chapter 9 to both profile your needs-based segments and create screening questions for targeting.
- **Calibrate your depth to your capacity.** Following all guidance thoroughly could produce 100+ need statements. Functional needs from the core job map are non-negotiable. Everything else should be calibrated to your team's strategic priorities and practical constraints.
