This chapter introduces Maximum Difference Scaling, or MaxDiff, as a reliable and user-friendly method for figuring out what your customers' unmet needs are. I will walk through how it directly addresses the problems with the traditional opportunity algorithm discussed in Chapter 7, walk you through a complete hands-on guide to using it, and then look beyond MaxDiff at complementary methods that fill the gaps it cannot.

## Review of Chapter 7

In the last chapter, we identified seven categories of concern with the traditional ODI opportunity scoring algorithm:

- **Double-weighting of importance** in the formula, which systematically suppresses satisfaction gaps and inflates the priority of high-importance needs regardless of how well they're being served.
- **Top-two-box information loss**, which discards meaningful differences between respondents by collapsing a 5-point scale into a binary "high/not high" classification.
- **Survey fatigue** from asking respondents to evaluate 100+ need statements, each requiring both an importance and a satisfaction rating, triggering the satisficing behaviors documented in Krosnick's research.
- **Bias amplification**, where the algorithm magnifies acquiescence bias, social desirability bias, negativity bias in satisfaction ratings, and cultural response style differences rather than correcting for them.
- **The table-stakes blind spot**, where the algorithm structurally cannot distinguish between needs that are safely met and can be ignored versus needs that are safely met and must be protected at all costs.
- **The non-functional needs problem**, where a quantification method designed for functional need statements doesn't adequately handle the emotional, social, and contextual need types covered in Chapter 6.
- **The validation gap**, where no independently verified evidence exists that the formula predicts innovation success better than simpler alternatives.

These are serious methodological concerns. But the goal of systematically identifying which customer needs deserve the most attention remains. The question is whether better tools exist.

The answer is to stop asking customers for abstract ratings and start asking them to make realistic trade-offs. This is the simple idea behind **Maximum Difference Scaling**, or **MaxDiff**. Instead of asking a customer to rate the importance of 100 different need statements on a five-point scale, MaxDiff presents a much simpler request. It shows people small sets of items and asks them to make a straightforward choice: "Of this list, which is the most important and which is the least important?"

This one change addresses many of the flaws of the old approach. By forcing a choice, MaxDiff avoids many of the biases that come with rating scales and gives you a true hierarchy of priorities. It mimics how people make decisions in the real world: by comparing options and deciding what matters more. The result is a cleaner, more reliable, and more precise picture of what your customers truly value.

But MaxDiff is not a silver bullet. It has its own limitations, and it works best as part of a broader research toolkit. This chapter is your guide to MaxDiff and the complementary methods that round out a practical quantification strategy.

## What is MaxDiff? A Simple Explanation

At its heart, MaxDiff is a way to understand what really matters to people by asking them to make simple, repeated choices. It breaks down the overwhelming task of rating many items into something much more manageable and human. [37]

### The Basic Idea

Imagine you want to know which iPhone features a group of friends thinks are most important. The old survey approach would be to list several features and ask your friends to rate each one on a scale of 1 to 5.

You would almost certainly get a lot of 4s and 5s for popular items like "battery life," "high-quality camera," and "faster processor," but you wouldn't know which of those is the _most_ important.

Formally known as Best-Worst Scaling and developed by Jordan Louviere, MaxDiff works differently. [24] Instead of that long list, you would show your friends just four or five features at a time and ask a simple question: "Of these options, which is the MOST interesting to you, and which is the LEAST interesting to you?"

You would then show them a few more sets with different combinations of features. After a dozen or so of these simple choices, a Hierarchical Bayesian (HB) analysis running in the background can figure out a complete, ranked preference list for every single feature for each person. The final result is a chart that clearly shows what people value most.

This chart shows what are called "utility scores," which are preference rankings derived from all those best-and-worst choices. Here, "Battery life that lasts all day" is the clear winner with a score of 19.55. This immediately tells product managers where to focus. The data also shows smaller differences, "Expandable storage" (14.11) and "High-quality camera" (13.92) are close, suggesting they are competing for a similar level of customer interest. This level of detail helps teams make much smarter trade-off decisions.

## How MaxDiff Addresses the Chapter 7 Concerns

MaxDiff directly solves several of the problems we identified, but it's important to be precise about which ones it addresses and which ones it doesn't.

**It eliminates the double-weighting problem**
There is no formula combining two separate dimensions. MaxDiff produces a single set of utility scores derived from forced choices. There's no opportunity for one dimension to mathematically dominate another, because there's only one dimension in the output.

**It avoids top-two-box information loss**
MaxDiff doesn't use rating scales at all, so there's no conversion from a 5-point scale to a binary classification. Every choice a respondent makes contributes to the model. The HB analysis uses all available information to estimate each person's preference structure, making fine distinctions between items that traditional top-two-box scoring cannot.

**It dramatically reduces survey fatigue**
Rating 100 different items is boring and exhausting. MaxDiff turns this into a more engaging, puzzle-like task. Each question is a new decision, which keeps people more focused. This leads to higher completion rates and better data quality from start to finish. A MaxDiff exercise evaluating 30 items typically requires only 10–12 choice sets and takes 5–8 minutes, compared to the 25–30 minutes Strategyn advocates for their rating-based surveys.

**It _reduces_ bias**
MaxDiff eliminates acquiescence bias entirely, because there's no scale to agree with. There's no "4" or "5" to default to. Cultural response style differences (tendency toward extremes vs. midpoints) also disappear, since respondents are choosing between items, not placing items on a scale.

However, **social desirability bias persists**. When respondents see "support businesses aligned with personal values" alongside "obtain coffee quickly," some will still pick the virtuous-sounding option as "most important" even if speed actually drives their behavior. MaxDiff forces a choice, but it can't force an honest one. This is an inherent limitation of any self-report method, and it's why behavioral validation (discussed later in this chapter) matters.

**It does not solve the table-stakes blind spot.** This is important to acknowledge. Standard MaxDiff produces relative rankings. Meaning it tells you Item A is preferred over Item B, but it cannot tell you whether customers actually _care_ about Item A in an absolute sense. Every item gets a score, even items nobody values much; one of them will still rank highest. More critically, a need that is both highly important and highly satisfied, a table-stakes need, will rank low in a MaxDiff study framed around "unmet needs" or "biggest problems." The respondent isn't frustrated by it, so they don't select it. This is the same blind spot we critiqued in Chapter 7, and MaxDiff inherits it. We'll discuss how to address this with complementary methods later in the chapter.

**It partially addresses the non-functional needs problem**
MaxDiff can accommodate emotional, social, and financial need statements alongside functional ones. Because respondents are making forced comparisons rather than rating each item independently, they're more likely to reveal genuine preferences even for abstract items. However, the comparison itself may feel artificial when items span very different categories. "Minimize anxiety about making the wrong choice" next to "minimize the time to find flights" asks the respondent to compare apples and oranges. Careful statement design and the Relevant Items MaxDiff variant (discussed below) can help manage this tension.

**It does not resolve the validation gap**
MaxDiff is a better measurement tool, but like ODI's opportunity scores, MaxDiff utility scores have not been independently validated as predictors of innovation success. They are a more reliable way to capture stated preferences, but stated preferences are still not the same as market behavior. This gap is why the complementary methods discussed at the end of this chapter matter.

## The Measurement Decision: What Should Your MaxDiff Actually Measure?

Before diving into building a MaxDiff survey, we have to address a key decision. The value of your MaxDiff output depends entirely on what dimension you ask people to evaluate.

Traditional ODI requires measuring both importance and satisfaction for every need, then combining them through the opportunity algorithm. We've already discussed the problems with that approach. MaxDiff solves the fatigue problem by using forced choices instead of ratings, but you still face a decision about what dimension to measure and this decision matters more than you might think.

### The "Importance" Trap

When trying to understand customer needs, our first instinct is often to ask about importance: "Which of these needs is most important to you?" Unfortunately, this usually leads to a "ceiling effect," where almost everything is rated as highly important and you cannot tell what to focus on.

For example, imagine a hospital trying to improve the patient experience. If they ask patients to prioritize needs like these:

- Having medical staff take my symptoms seriously
- Getting diagnostic test results quickly
- Understanding my treatment plan clearly
- Having nurses respond promptly to requests

Almost every patient would say all of these are "important." They are all fundamental to good healthcare. The results would be a flat, undifferentiated list of priorities, giving the hospital no direction on where to improve.

In research, we call this the problem of "Stated Importance." When you ask people what they want, they say everything. This is why many researchers advocate moving toward "Derived Importance," where we uncover what matters by analyzing the choices people make rather than the ratings they give. [38]

MaxDiff helps with this by forcing trade-offs, but the framing of your question still matters enormously. Ask people which needs are "most important" and you may still get clustering at the top. Ask them which problems are "most frustrating" or which improvements would "make the biggest difference" and you often get much cleaner separation.

To avoid the importance trap and get actionable data, you have several realistic approaches to consider.

### Option 1: MaxDiff on Importance, Then Targeted Satisfaction

You run your MaxDiff study asking customers to identify which needs matter most to getting their job done. This gives you a clear hierarchy of importance. Then you add a short follow-up section. Not a second MaxDiff, just 10–15 simple satisfaction rating questions covering only the needs that ranked in your top tier.

This approach preserves the gap analysis logic of ODI while dramatically reducing survey burden. Instead of rating satisfaction on 100 items, customers only evaluate the 15–20 that the importance ranking identified as priorities. You get both dimensions without the exhaustion.

The downside is added survey length and complexity. Even a short satisfaction section adds time. You also need to design conditional logic so the satisfaction questions reflect each respondent's importance rankings, which requires more sophisticated survey programming.

**Choose this approach if:** You have a mature product with established value propositions and need to know not just where to innovate but what to protect. The extra survey complexity is worth it because a misstep is costly.

### Option 2: MaxDiff on Satisfaction Only

You run your MaxDiff asking customers to identify which needs are most poorly served by current solutions, or which problems are biggest. This captures dissatisfaction directly through forced choice.

Customers will not identify something as a major problem unless it matters to them. If someone doesn't care about a capability, they're unlikely to flag it as their biggest pain point even if current solutions handle it poorly. Dissatisfaction, the argument goes, implicitly signals importance.

This is often true, but not always. There are scenarios where satisfaction alone can mislead you.

A customer might express dissatisfaction with an aspect of the experience they rarely encounter and don't actually value. Perhaps they once tried to access their account from a shared device, had a poor experience, and now report dissatisfaction, but this edge case doesn't reflect their regular usage or priorities. If you only measure satisfaction, this noise can look like signal.

More significantly, satisfaction-only measurement makes it difficult to identify your **table stakes**. These are things you must not regress on. High satisfaction might tempt you to deprioritize maintenance or take quality for granted, not realizing that any degradation would cause damage. Without some importance signal, you lose visibility into what you need to protect, not just what you need to fix.

**Choose this approach if:** You are focused purely on identifying pain points and have other signals (support tickets, churn analysis, customer health scores) to help you understand what existing value you need to protect.

### Option 3: Combined Framing (Recommended for Most Teams)

The most practical approach for most teams is to frame your MaxDiff question in a way that captures both dimensions simultaneously.

Instead of asking "Which of these is most important?" or "Which of these are you least satisfied with?", you ask something like:

- "Which of these unmet needs would make the biggest difference if solved?"
- "Which of these is the most important problem you currently face?"
- "Which of these improvements would have the greatest impact on your ability to get your job done?"

This framing implies both importance (it would "make a difference" or have "impact") and dissatisfaction (it is an "unmet need" or "problem"). You're asking customers to prioritize based on the gap between what matters and what's working, which is exactly what the opportunity concept tries to capture but in a single, natural question.

The trade-off is that you lose the ability to cleanly separate the two dimensions. You cannot say with precision "this need is highly important but already satisfied" versus "this need is moderately important but completely unmet." The combined framing blends these into a single priority signal.

**A candid note on table stakes:** The combined framing inherits the same table-stakes blind spot discussed earlier. A need that is critically important but already well-served will rank low because it's not an "unmet need" or "problem." For most product decisions, this is acceptable. But if protecting existing value is critical for your product (as it often is for mature products), you'll need to supplement this approach with behavioral data or a targeted follow-up. The "Beyond MaxDiff" section at the end of this chapter discusses how.

**Choose this approach if:** You are an early-stage product still searching for product-market fit, or you need to identify the most important unmet needs quickly. The nuance of separating importance from satisfaction matters less than speed and clarity.

### Option 4: Relevant Items MaxDiff

When your need list is large and relevance varies across respondents, Relevant Items MaxDiff offers an elegant solution. Before the MaxDiff exercise begins, respondents complete a quick screener indicating which needs are actually relevant to their situation. The MaxDiff tasks are then built only from those relevant items.

For example, if you're researching the job of "plan a vacation," a business traveler might indicate that needs related to "entertaining children during travel" or "finding family-friendly accommodations" aren't relevant. Those needs would be excluded from their MaxDiff exercise entirely, reducing cognitive load and eliminating noise from forced evaluations of irrelevant items.

This approach offers several advantages:

- **Reduced respondent burden.** Instead of evaluating 80 needs, a respondent might only see the 25–30 that apply to them.
- **Cleaner data.** You avoid the noise that comes from respondents guessing or satisficing on needs they've never experienced.
- **Relevance as signal.** The selection of relevant items itself becomes valuable data. You can analyze which needs different user types consider relevant before even looking at the utility scores.

**Handling the analysis**
When running HB estimation on Relevant Items MaxDiff data, you need to decide how to treat items that were not shown. If respondents explicitly marked items as "not relevant," you would typically use the "Missing Inferior" setting, which treats those items as systematically less preferred than the items they did select. This prevents the model from imputing neutral or positive utilities for needs the respondent indicated don't apply to them. **Be careful here:** if you accidentally use "Missing at Random" instead of "Missing Inferior," you'd impute neutral utilities for items the respondent explicitly said were irrelevant.

**The segmentation trade-off**
Traditional MaxDiff segmentation clusters respondents based on their utility scores across identical item sets. With Relevant Items MaxDiff, segmentation works differently:

- You can segment based on **relevance patterns**: which needs did different user types select as relevant? This can reveal fundamentally different job contexts or user segments before you even analyze preferences.
- You can segment based on **utilities within shared items**: for respondents who selected overlapping relevant items, you can still cluster based on how they prioritized those shared needs.
- You may need to **combine approaches**: use relevance patterns for initial segmentation, then analyze utility differences within each segment.

This is generally messier than traditional MaxDiff segmentation. If two respondents have no overlapping relevant items, you cannot directly compare their preferences. You'd need to rely on the relevance selection patterns or external variables (demographics, behaviors) to group them.

**Choose this approach if:** You have a large needs list (50+) where relevance genuinely varies by user type, you're comfortable with segmentation based on relevance patterns rather than pure utility comparisons, and you suspect your market contains fundamentally different user segments with different job contexts. It's particularly powerful when the relevance selection itself is strategically interesting. For example, discovering that enterprise buyers consider an entirely different set of needs relevant than SMB buyers.

**Be cautious if:** You need clean utility comparisons across your entire sample, your segmentation strategy depends on clustering everyone based on the same items, or your needs are universally relevant to anyone doing the job.

### Option 5: Feature-Based MaxDiff

If your team has already moved past discovery and has a concrete list of feature concepts, you can skip the needs layer entirely and ask customers to prioritize features directly.

The question becomes straightforward: "Which of these features would you most want us to build?" or "Which of these improvements would be most valuable to you?"

This approach offers practical advantages. The output is immediately **actionable**. A prioritized feature list that can directly inform your roadmap without additional translation. It speaks the language of stakeholders. Engineers, executives, and product managers naturally think in features. And it reduces abstraction by avoiding the sometimes awkward process of translating ranked needs into features after the research.

However, this approach has clear limitations. You're testing solutions, not problems. If a feature ranks low, you can't tell whether the underlying need is unimportant or whether your feature concept simply failed to resonate. You might abandon a valuable opportunity space because your first solution idea was weak. You also constrain your innovation space. Needs-based research can reveal opportunities you haven't considered, while feature-based research only validates or invalidates ideas you've already generated. And features often bundle multiple needs, making it hard to interpret what the ranking actually tells you about underlying priorities.

**Choose this approach if:** You're in execution mode rather than discovery mode, you have high confidence that your feature concepts are good solutions to real needs, and you need to prioritize a backlog quickly. This works best as a complement to earlier needs-based research, not a replacement for it.

**Be cautious if:** You're still in early discovery, you want to understand the problem space before committing to solutions, or you suspect your current feature ideas might not be the best ways to address customer needs.

> **Author's Note:** I would be cautious about treating feature-based MaxDiff as equivalent to the other four options. Options 1–4 are all variations on measuring needs, with different framings around importance, satisfaction, or relevance. Feature-based MaxDiff measures something fundamentally different: preference for proposed solutions. Options 1–4 help you figure out _where to focus_. Feature-based MaxDiff helps you figure out _how to execute_ once you've already decided where to focus. They answer different questions at different stages of the product development process.

### Anchored MaxDiff: Adding Absolute Thresholds

One limitation shared by all standard MaxDiff variants is that the output is purely relative. It tells you Item A is preferred over Item B, but not whether anyone actually cares about Item A in an absolute sense. In a standard MaxDiff, one item will always rank highest, even if every item on your list is trivial to the customer.

Anchored MaxDiff addresses this by adding a follow-up step after the choice tasks. Respondents indicate, for each item or for a subset, whether the item is "must have," "nice to have," or "not needed" (or a similar absolute threshold scale). This anchoring step converts the relative utility scores into something closer to absolute preference, letting you draw a line between "customers genuinely want this" and "this ranks highest but nobody really cares."

This is particularly valuable for the table-stakes problem. An item that ranks low in the relative MaxDiff exercise but gets flagged as "must have" in the anchoring step is a clear table-stakes need.

If your platform supports it, Anchored MaxDiff is worth considering whenever you need to distinguish between "most preferred among these options" and "actually important in the real world." It adds modest survey length but provides a dimension of insight that standard MaxDiff cannot.

_A note on feature-based MaxDiff_: If you've already completed needs-based discovery and want to prioritize a backlog of feature concepts, you can run MaxDiff on features directly. This gives you immediately actionable output but answers a different question — "which solution should we build?" rather than "which problem should we solve?" Use this as a follow-up to needs research, not a replacement for it.

### Why This Changes Prioritization Conversations

Regardless of which option you choose, MaxDiff outputs create different conversations than traditional ODI scores.

Traditional ODI outputs create a specific problem in prioritization meetings. When you present opportunity scores like 14.7 versus 12.3, stakeholders inevitably ask whether that difference is meaningful. Is a 2.4-point gap worth reorganizing the roadmap? The math looks precise, but as we discussed in Chapter 7, that precision is largely illusory. The honest answer to "does this difference matter?" is usually "it depends," which undermines confidence in the data and opens the door for whoever argues loudest.

MaxDiff outputs sidestep this problem. Instead of debating point differences, you can make a cleaner statement. When forced to choose, customers consistently ranked "data export reliability" above "collaborative editing features." The hierarchy is the insight, not the precise numerical distance between items. You're not claiming that export reliability is exactly 1.4 times more important. You're claiming it wins head-to-head matchups more often, which is a more defensible and intuitive statement.

This changes how prioritization debates happen. With traditional ODI scores, teams often get stuck arguing about methodology. Is the algorithm right? Is 14.7 really different from 12.3? Should we trust the survey? With ranked preferences from forced choices, the conversation shifts to strategic questions that matter:

- Should we address the top-ranked need first, or is there a cluster of related needs in positions two through five that we could solve together more efficiently?
- The third-ranked need is technically lower priority, but it's much easier to build. Could we capture a quick win while we plan the larger effort?
- Our enterprise segment shows different rankings than our SMB segment. Should we build different solutions or find the common thread?

These are productive debates about strategy and resources, not arguments about surveys. The research should inform decisions, not become the subject of decisions.

## The Statement Syntax Decision: Solution-Focused vs. Traditional JTBD Need Statements

Once you've decided what dimension to measure, you face a second decision, how to write the actual statements. This brings us to a tension between the rigorous principles of Jobs-to-be-Done theory and the practical realities of survey design.

A core tenet of JTBD is to remain solution-agnostic, focusing exclusively on the customer's desired need or outcome. However, to get clear, quantifiable data from a survey, we sometimes need to bend this rule for the sake of clarity.

This is a practical trade-off you often have to make when quantifying needs. JTBD-focused need statements are the standard for discovery research, where you're mapping out the job for the first time. But in a quantitative survey, these abstract statements can be hard for people to evaluate, often leading to the ceiling effect where everything seems equally important. To get a clear signal on priorities, you sometimes need to frame the needs in a more concrete way that grounds the respondent in their actual experience.

### Option A: Use Solution-Focused Statements

This approach sacrifices theory for practical clarity. It makes the survey much easier for people to answer and gives you clear, actionable insights about performance gaps.

Let's look back at our hospital example. The traditional JTBD need statement might be:

> Minimize the time spent waiting for the results of a diagnostic test.

We could change it to the more solution-focused statement:

> I receive the results of diagnostic tests in a timely manner.

The second version is far easier for a patient to evaluate. They can think back to their actual experience and quickly decide if they got their blood test results when they expected them. This grounds the question in reality and reduces the mental effort required to answer.

You might notice that "receiving results" sounds like an activity rather than a need. Strict ODI practitioners may argue this violates the rules of defining a need. While true, the statement remains neutral regarding the solution. It doesn't mention an app, a phone call, or a paper letter. It simply describes the successful completion of the step. In a survey context, the clarity for the respondent is worth this minor shift in language.

**Choose this approach if:** Your goal is to identify and prioritize improvements for an existing product or service. It gives you a clear roadmap for optimization.

### Option B: Use Traditional Need Statements in a Narrow Context

This approach sticks much closer to JTBD principles. To make abstract needs comparable, you narrow your focus to a theme or step within the larger job, such as "managing a treatment plan." Within this tighter context, you can quantify more granular, tactical needs that are still solution-agnostic. When all the statements relate to the same focused activity, respondents can make more meaningful trade-offs.

For example, if the theme was "understanding the treatment plan," your MaxDiff statements might look like this using the classic, direction-based JTBD syntax:

- Minimize the time it takes to get my questions about the plan answered by a doctor.
- Minimize the confusion caused by medical jargon used by staff.
- Minimize the difficulty of remembering all the steps in my treatment plan.
- Minimize the likelihood of feeling rushed when discussing the plan.

As we discussed in Chapter 6 with JTBD and ODI syntax, if this rigid syntax feels too restrictive or unnatural for a survey, you can rephrase these statements using more conversational language. The goal is to remain focused on the need, not the solution. Here's how the same needs could be written in a more flexible style:

- Quickly get my questions about the plan answered by a doctor.
- Avoid confusion from the medical jargon used by staff.
- Easily remember all the steps in my treatment plan.
- Avoid feeling rushed when discussing the plan.

Notice that in both formats, all of these statements are traditional need statements. They describe what the patient wants to achieve without mentioning a solution, but they're all related to a particular part of the patient's journey (a job step in the job map). This narrow focus makes the trade-off ("What is more frustrating: the medical jargon or feeling rushed?") a realistic choice for the respondent.

**Choose this approach if:** You're doing foundational research to deeply understand a part of the customer's job. It's ideal for uncovering opportunities for breakthrough innovation rather than just incremental improvements.

### Combining the Two Decisions

To summarize, you're making two independent decisions when designing your MaxDiff:

1. **What dimension to measure:** Importance only, satisfaction only, or combined framing.
2. **How to write statements:** Solution-focused for clarity, or traditional JTBD need syntax for methodological rigor.

These choices are orthogonal. You can use combined framing with solution-focused statements (practical and clear) or combined framing with traditional JTBD need statements (rigorous but requires narrow scope). The right combination depends on your research goals, your product's maturity, and the cognitive load you're willing to place on respondents.

The broader point is that there is **no single correct approach**. The original ODI methodology presents itself as a precise system, but as we've seen throughout this book, that precision often obscures judgment calls and trade-offs. MaxDiff is a better tool, but it's still a tool. You have to decide how to wield it based on your context, resources, and risk tolerance.

What matters is that you're measuring customer needs through forced trade-offs rather than inflated ratings, that you're producing a clear hierarchy rather than a spreadsheet of similar-looking scores, and that you're designing your research to answer the strategic questions your team actually faces.

## A Practical Guide to Building Your MaxDiff Study

With those decisions made, we can walk through the mechanics of creating an effective MaxDiff study. Getting this right comes down to three things: the study design, the quality of your statements, and the experience you create for the person taking the survey.

A quick note on tools: you'll find many great platforms out there to run a MaxDiff study, including tools like Qualtrics, Sawtooth Software, Conjointly, and others. Because the specific buttons you click and the exact setup menus change over time and differ between platforms, this guide won't be a detailed tutorial for any single piece of software. Instead, we'll focus on the universal principles and platform-agnostic steps that are essential for a successful study, no matter which tool you choose.

The fundamentals of research design are what truly drive good results, and mastering them will allow you to confidently set up your study on any platform.

### Step 1: Write and Test Your Statements

The quality of your data is entirely dependent on the quality of the statements you test.

**Write Clear Statements**
Each statement should be short, clear, and contain only one idea. Instead of a complex statement like "A15 Bionic chip with 6-core CPU for faster machine learning," break it down into benefits like "Fast performance for demanding apps" or "Quickly switches between apps."

**Ensure They Can Be Compared**
All statements in your list must make sense when compared with each other. A person should be able to make a meaningful trade-off between any two statements on your list. If you're mixing functional, emotional, and financial needs in the same exercise, pilot test carefully to ensure respondents don't find the comparisons nonsensical.

**Pilot Test Everything**
Before you launch your study, test your statements. First, have your internal team take the survey. This will catch obvious clarity issues. If you find yourself rereading a statement, your customers will too. After an internal review, test the survey with 20 to 30 people from your target audience. Ask them what they think each statement means and if they found any choices difficult or confusing. Use their feedback to refine your list before the full launch.

### Step 2: Design the Study

The statistical setup for MaxDiff is more forgiving than many other methods, but you need to get a few parameters right.

#### Sample Size: How Many People Do You Really Need?

The goal with sample size is to reach a point of **stability**, where adding more respondents doesn't meaningfully change the overall ranking of your items.

**The Baseline (200 Respondents).** A sample of 200 is considered a strong baseline because it typically provides enough data to create narrow confidence intervals around your scores. With 200 respondents, you can be more confident that a 5-point difference between items is statistically real and not just random noise.

**The Practical Minimum (50–75 Respondents).** Why can MaxDiff work with smaller samples? The answer lies in the Hierarchical Bayes (HB) analysis used to calculate the scores. HB is a sophisticated model that estimates scores for each individual while simultaneously learning from the patterns of the entire group. In simple terms, it "borrows strength" across respondents. If one person's answers are a bit inconsistent, the model uses data from other, similar people to improve its estimate for that individual. This makes the data from each person more powerful, allowing you to get good directional insights, knowing the top 5 items, for instance even with a smaller group. The trade-off is that your confidence intervals will be wider, so you'll have less precision in the final scores.

**Segmentation (50+ Per Group).** When you want to compare different groups of customers, you should treat each group as a mini-study. Aiming for at least 50 people _per segment_ ensures you have enough data to get a reliable read on that group's priorities. If you plan to analyze four segments, a total sample of 200 (50 for each) would be your minimum starting point but be aware that this is the _floor_. Sawtooth Software's guidance suggests that individual-level HB estimates can be less stable with very small segment sizes, so if precise within-segment rankings are critical to your decisions, aim higher. A sample of 75–100 per segment provides a more comfortable margin.

Note on calculations: The sample sizes listed here are practical rules of thumb that work for the vast majority of commercial projects. If you need to calculate exact power requirements for a complex academic study, I recommend reviewing the technical resources provided by Sawtooth Software. [39]

#### Choice Set Configuration

This is about managing the **cognitive load** on your respondents to ensure you get high-quality data from beginning to end.

**Items Per Set (4 to 5).** This range is the sweet spot for human decision-making. When presented with 4 or 5 options, a person can reasonably hold them all in working memory to make a comparative judgment. If you show 7 or 8 items at once, people get overwhelmed and often resort to mental shortcuts, and the quality of their choices declines.

**Sets Per Respondent (6 to 8).** The biggest threat to data quality in any survey is respondent fatigue. While the MaxDiff task is more engaging than rating scales, it's still repetitive. Experience shows that after about 8 sets, many people start answering on autopilot. Keeping the task to 6–8 sets ensures you capture high-quality, considered choices from each person. The HB analysis method is efficient enough to build a solid model from this amount of data.

#### Statistical Coverage: Ensuring a Fair and Accurate Test

Your survey software doesn't just show random items; it follows a precise **experimental design** to ensure the results are accurate and unbiased.

**The "Round Robin" Principle.** The core goal of the design is to generate enough direct comparisons to build a reliable model. To do this, the design ensures that, across all respondents, every single statement appears in the same set with every other statement multiple times. Think of it like a sports tournament: to get a true ranking, you want every team to play every other team. Since one person can't do all those comparisons, the experimental design spreads these "matchups" intelligently across the entire sample.

**Balance and Orthogonality.** A good design follows two key principles. First is balance, meaning every item is shown a roughly equal number of times overall. Second is orthogonality, which ensures the items are shown together in a way that lets the model tell their individual preferences apart, preventing items from always appearing with the same "partner."

You don't need to build this design yourself. Modern survey platforms handle it automatically. But knowing these principles helps you understand that the background process is a structured, scientific approach designed to give you the cleanest possible read on what your customers value.

### Step 3: Build the Survey Experience

How you present the survey to a respondent can dramatically affect the quality of the data you get back.

#### Set the Context: Grounding Your Respondent

This is the first thing your respondent should see. Its job is to activate the right memories and put them in the correct frame of mind for your questions. Without proper context, people will answer based on abstract feelings rather than specific, relevant experiences.

**Practical Examples:**

- **For B2B Software:** "Please think about your typical process for completing [Job to be Done, e.g., your monthly expense report] using [Software Name]. The following questions will be about that experience."
- **For a Retail Store:** "We'd like you to think about your most recent shopping trip to [Store Name]. Please keep that visit in mind as you answer the next few questions."
- **For a Travel Website:** "Please reflect on the last time you booked a personal trip online. We're interested in understanding what was most and least important to you during that booking process."
- **For a Healthcare Experience:** "Thinking about your last check-up with your primary care doctor, please consider all aspects of that visit, from scheduling to the appointment itself."

**Tip:** Use recency to your advantage. Asking about "your last visit" or "your experience in the past month" is usually more effective than asking about their experience in general, as it prompts a more vivid and accurate memory.

#### Give Simple Instructions: Clarity Over Complexity

Your respondents don't need to know the name of the methodology or the statistics behind it. The goal is to make the task feel effortless.

**Examples of Effective Wording:**

- **Standard & Clear:** "On each of the next few screens, you will see a group of statements. From each group, please choose the **one** that is **MOST** important to you and the **one** that is **LEAST** important to you. There are no right or wrong answers; we want to understand what matters most to you."
- **Short & Casual:** "In each set, just pick your top choice and your bottom choice. That's it!"

**Tip:** Explicitly tell respondents to **only consider the items on the screen**. This prevents them from trying to compare an item in the current set to one they saw two sets ago.

### Step 4: Choose Your Platform

Several tools can help you run a MaxDiff study, each with its own strengths:

- **Qualtrics:** One of the most accessible options, especially if you already use it. It has a built-in MaxDiff question type that handles the design for you. [35] The analysis tools are basic, so you may need to export your data, but it's a great choice for straightforward studies.
- **Sawtooth Software:** The gold standard for choice-based research, offering sophisticated design and analysis tools. [34] It has a steeper learning curve and costs more, but it's the right choice for complex or large-scale projects.
- **Other Platforms:** Tools like Conjointly offer a good middle ground, with more advanced analysis than Qualtrics but a more user-friendly interface than Sawtooth.

No matter which platform you choose, make sure to test your survey on both desktop and mobile devices to ensure it works smoothly for everyone.

### Step 5: Fielding Your Survey

Launching your survey is a major milestone, but your work isn't done yet. How you find, motivate, and manage your respondents will directly impact your timeline, budget, and the trustworthiness of your data.

#### Planning Your Recruitment and Fielding Strategy

**1. Define Your Audience and Design an Effective Screener.** Be crystal clear about who you need to hear from. Are they customers who have used a particular feature in the last 90 days? Are they people in a certain industry who don't use your product? The criteria you set will be turned into a short screener questionnaire to ensure only qualified people participate.

Design your screener questions carefully to avoid giving away the "right" answer. For example, instead of asking "Do you use our advanced reporting feature?" ask "Which of the following features have you used in the past 90 days?" and include your target feature among a list of other plausible options.

**2. Estimate Incidence Rate (IR) and Set a Budget.** Your screener criteria will determine your Incidence Rate (IR) — the percentage of a general population that will qualify for your study. A high IR (50%+) means a broad audience and easier, more affordable recruitment. A low IR (5% or less) means a niche audience that's more difficult and expensive to reach.

**3. Choose Your Recruitment Method.** Options include your own customer lists (affordable, highly relevant, but risks sampling bias), third-party research panels (fast and diverse, but more expensive and requires quality vigilance), and targeted digital channels like LinkedIn or Facebook ads (effective for niche audiences, but harder to predict cost and timeline).

**4. Determine the Right Incentives.** A fair incentive signals that you value respondents' time. Cash-equivalent rewards like gift cards are usually most effective. A common rule of thumb for general consumer audiences is $1–$2 for every five minutes of survey time. For highly paid professionals, you'll need to offer more. Be careful not to over-incentivize, as unusually high rewards can attract fraudulent respondents.

**5. Craft Your Invitation.** Your invitation should clearly and concisely state the purpose of the study, the estimated time to complete it, the incentive offered, a statement on confidentiality, and a contact link for technical issues.

#### Executing the Launch and Monitoring Quality

**Pre-test again before launching.**

**Start with a Soft Launch**
Before sending your survey to your entire sample, launch it to 5–10% of your target audience. Review these initial responses carefully to catch technical glitches, confusing wording, or display problems on different devices.

**Actively Check for Bad Respondents**
It's standard practice to identify and remove responses from people who aren't giving thoughtful answers:

- **Speeders:** People who complete the survey so fast they couldn't have read the questions.
- **Inattentive Responders:** People who fail attention check questions.
- **Patterned Responders:** People who always choose the first and last items in every set.

**Manage Your Quotas**
If your study requires input from specific customer segments, monitor your incoming data to ensure you're meeting these targets.

**Clean Your Final Dataset**
Removing speeders, inattentive respondents, and patterned responders is a non-negotiable final step before analysis. This is what makes your insights trustworthy and defensible.

### Labeling Your Results Correctly

Before we dive into analysis, let's clarify an important point about interpretation. Your analysis software will likely call the final numbers "utility scores." This is a generic statistical term, it doesn't mean the numbers represent economic value or importance. **The meaning of the score depends entirely on the question you asked.**

If you asked respondents which items they were most and least _satisfied_ with, these numbers are Relative Satisfaction Scores. A high score means that item is working well for the customer. A low score signals a problem area.

If you asked which unmet needs would "make the biggest difference," these are Relative Priority Scores (a blend of importance and dissatisfaction).

**Label your charts to match your framing**
If you label a chart "Importance" when you actually measured satisfaction, you will confuse your stakeholders. A low score on a satisfaction chart doesn't mean the item is unimportant. Throughout the following example, we'll use a combined framing ("most important unmet need") and label our output accordingly.

### Interpreting Patterns in Your Results

Whether you ran the R code yourself or received results from a researcher, the next step is the same: making sense of the patterns. This is where most MaxDiff guides stop. They show you the chart and leave you to figure out what to do with it. Here's how to read these results strategically.

#### Identify Tiers, Not Just Rankings

The most useful way to read MaxDiff output is not as a strict rank order (1st, 2nd, 3rd...) but as a set of **tiers**. Groups of needs that are meaningfully different from each other.

Look at the confidence interval chart. When two attributes have overlapping confidence intervals, you cannot confidently say one is more important than the other. They belong in the same tier. When there's a clear gap between one group and the next you've found a tier boundary.

In our coffee shop example, the data suggests roughly three tiers:

- **Tier 1 (Clear priorities):** Rewards programs, coffee quality, and price. These have the highest utility scores and their confidence intervals are separated from the rest. This is where investment will have the most impact.
- **Tier 2 (The competitive middle):** Location convenience, off-peak availability, comfortable spaces, values alignment, guidance on selection, internet access, and menu variety. These cluster together with largely overlapping confidence intervals. Individually, none of these is significantly more important than the others but collectively, they represent the "good enough" expectations that shape which coffee shop people choose on a given day.
- **Tier 3 (Lower priorities):** Speed, food options, quiet spaces, hygiene, and mobile ordering. These score lowest and are separated from the middle tier.

This tier structure changes the conversation. Instead of debating whether to invest in "values alignment" (6.80) or "selection guidance" (6.79) (a meaningless distinction given the data) your team can have a more productive discussion: "How do we win in Tier 1, maintain competitiveness in Tier 2, and decide which Tier 3 items we can safely deprioritize?"

#### Watch for Surprising Rankings

The data sometimes reveals priorities your team wouldn't have predicted, and these surprises are often the most valuable findings.

In our example, "Experience service in hygienic conditions" scores near the bottom. Does this mean customers don't care about hygiene? Almost certainly not. It likely means hygiene is a table-stakes need. Meaning customers expect it, it's currently well-met, and they wouldn't select it as their "most important unmet need" because it isn't unmet. This is exactly the table-stakes blind spot we discussed earlier.

Similarly, if "Support businesses aligned with personal values" scores moderately high, resist the temptation to over-invest based on this alone. This is a category where social desirability bias is most likely to inflate scores. Respondents may select it as "most important" because it sounds like the right answer, not because it actually drives their choice of coffee shop.

When you encounter surprising results, the right response is not to dismiss them but to investigate further. Cross-reference against behavioral data, support tickets, churn analysis, or follow-up interviews to validate whether the ranking reflects genuine customer behavior or measurement artifacts.

#### What MaxDiff Cannot Tell You

It's worth being explicit about what you should _not_ try to extract from MaxDiff utility scores:

**Don't treat small score differences as meaningful.** A score of 6.87 vs. 6.80 is not a meaningful difference. Use the confidence intervals to determine which differences are real. If two items' CIs overlap, treat them as equivalent in priority.

**Don't use MaxDiff to estimate willingness to pay.** Utility scores tell you relative preference, not economic value. A need with twice the utility score is not worth twice the investment. For pricing and willingness-to-pay questions, you need conjoint analysis or Van Westendorp pricing studies, which are different tools designed for different questions.

**Don't assume the ranking is stable over time.** MaxDiff captures a snapshot of priorities at the moment of the survey. Customer needs shift as competitive landscapes change, as your product evolves, and as market conditions fluctuate. Treat the results as a current-state assessment, not a permanent truth.

**Don't skip the "why."** MaxDiff tells you _what_ customers prioritize but not _why_. If "rewards programs" rank highest, you still don't know whether customers want points, discounts, exclusive access, or something else entirely. The quantitative ranking should inform where to focus your qualitative research, not replace it.

## Beyond MaxDiff: Complementary Methods

MaxDiff is a powerful tool for prioritizing needs, but it's one tool. A complete quantification strategy uses multiple methods that answer different questions at different stages of the product development process. This section covers the most important complements to MaxDiff.

### Key Driver Analysis: What Actually Drives Outcomes

Key Driver Analysis (KDA), also known as derived importance analysis, uses regression to identify which aspects of the customer experience actually predict overall satisfaction, loyalty, or whatever outcome metric matters most to your business.

The approach is straightforward. You collect satisfaction ratings on individual needs (these can be simple 5-point ratings. The survey is short because you're not rating 100+ items) plus an overall outcome metric like overall satisfaction, likelihood to recommend, or likelihood to renew. Then you run a regression to see which individual ratings most strongly predict the overall score. The needs with the highest regression coefficients are the ones that actually _drive_ the outcome, regardless of what people _say_ is important.

**Why this matters for your JTBD research:** KDA solves two problems that neither ODI's opportunity scores nor MaxDiff utility scores can address.

First, it reveals **derived importance** rather than stated importance. This directly addresses the social desirability bias we've discussed throughout this book. Customers may _say_ they care about sustainability or ethical sourcing and MaxDiff will dutifully rank these items wherever respondents place them. But if sustainability satisfaction has a near-zero regression coefficient in predicting overall satisfaction, you know it doesn't actually drive their behavior. The inverse is also true: a need that customers take for granted (and therefore rank low in MaxDiff) might turn out to be a strong predictor of loyalty.

Second, it identifies **table-stakes needs** through a different lens than MaxDiff. In a KDA plot, you can map each need on two axes: its regression coefficient (how strongly it drives the outcome) and its current satisfaction level. Needs with high coefficients and high current satisfaction are your table stakes. They drive outcomes but nobody's complaining about them _yet_. Needs with high coefficients and low satisfaction are your priority opportunities. Needs with low coefficients regardless of satisfaction can be safely deprioritized.

**Practical implementation:** KDA doesn't require specialized survey software. Any team with basic R or Python skills can run it on standard survey data they may already be collecting. A short survey with 15–20 satisfaction items plus one overall outcome question is typically sufficient. If you're already running CSAT or NPS surveys, you may be able to add a few targeted items and generate a KDA with minimal additional effort.

**The limitation:** KDA tells you what _currently_ drives satisfaction with _existing_ solutions. It doesn't surface unmet needs or opportunities for breakthrough innovation. That's what MaxDiff is for. The two methods answer different questions and are strongest when used together: MaxDiff identifies where customers want you to go, and KDA validates whether getting there actually moves the metrics that matter.

### Constant Sum: The Budget-Friendly Alternative

If your team doesn't have access to MaxDiff software, constant sum (also called budget allocation) is the simplest forced-trade-off method available. You give respondents a fixed budget, typically 100 points, and ask them to distribute it across needs based on priority.

Constant sum forces trade-offs like MaxDiff does: if you give 30 points to one need, you have fewer points for the rest. It runs in any basic survey tool, requires no experimental design or HB estimation, and the output is immediately interpretable.

However, It doesn't scale well past 8–10 items. For example distributing 100 points across 30 items is cognitively overwhelming. Respondents tend to spread points too evenly across items, dampening the signal. And unlike MaxDiff, it doesn't produce individual-level estimates.

For teams without access to Sawtooth or Qualtrics MaxDiff modules, constant sum is a meaningful improvement over independent Likert ratings for small-scale prioritization exercises. It's not as precise or scalable as MaxDiff, but it's better than the alternative of no forced trade-offs at all.

### TURF Analysis: Optimizing Your Roadmap

After MaxDiff gives you a ranked list of priorities, a natural product question is: "If we can only address three needs in the next release, which three cover the _most_ customers?"

TURF (Total Unduplicated Reach and Frequency) analysis answers this by finding the combination of items that maximizes the number of people whose top priority is addressed. It's not a standalone method it uses your MaxDiff data as input, but it solves a specific roadmap optimization problem.

For example, your MaxDiff data might show that the #1 need and the #2 need are the top priorities for largely the same group of customers. Addressing both gives you depth with one segment but doesn't expand your reach. TURF would identify that addressing the #1 need plus the #4 need covers a broader set of customers, even though #4 ranked lower overall.

Most MaxDiff analysis platforms (including Sawtooth Software) include TURF analysis capabilities. It's particularly useful when you have limited development capacity and need to maximize the impact of a small number of initiatives.

### Conjoint Analysis: Testing Solutions, Not Just Needs

While MaxDiff ranks individual items, conjoint analysis (specifically Choice-Based Conjoint, or CBC) tests _bundles_ of features and their interactions. It can tell you whether customers prefer a product with Feature A + Feature C over one with Feature B + Feature D and at what price point.

Conjoint answers a fundamentally different question than MaxDiff. MaxDiff asks "which problem should we solve?" Conjoint asks "what combination of solutions should we build, and what will customers pay for them?" This makes conjoint the natural next step after needs-based research, when your team has moved from discovery into design.

A detailed treatment of conjoint is beyond the scope of this chapter, but if your product decisions involve trade-offs between features, pricing tiers, or bundling strategies, it's worth exploring. Chapman and Rodden's _Quantitative User Experience Research_ provides accessible coverage of choice modeling methods including conjoint and MaxDiff. [40]

### The HEART Framework: Closing the Loop

All of the methods discussed so far, MaxDiff, KDA, constant sum, TURF, conjoint, are point-in-time research tools. You run a study, get results, and make decisions. But they don't tell you whether those decisions actually improved the customer experience. For that, you need an ongoing measurement framework.

The HEART framework, originated by Kerry Rodden at Google and detailed in Rodden's work with Chapman, provides a structured approach to tracking user experience outcomes over time. [41, 40] HEART stands for five dimensions of user experience:

- **Happiness:** User attitudes and satisfaction, typically measured via surveys (CSAT, NPS, or similar).
- **Engagement:** The level and depth of user interaction — how much and how meaningfully people use your product.
- **Adoption:** How many new users or new-feature users you're gaining.
- **Retention:** Whether existing users keep coming back.
- **Task Success:** How effectively users can accomplish their goals — measured through completion rates, error rates, or time-on-task.

The framework is operationalized through a Goals-Signals-Metrics (GSM) process. For each relevant HEART dimension, teams define overarching goals, identify signals that success or failure might manifest in user behavior or attitudes, and then develop specific, quantifiable metrics based on those signals.

**Why HEART belongs in this chapter:** Your MaxDiff study identifies which customer needs are most important and underserved. Your product team builds solutions to address those needs. HEART provides the tracking infrastructure to know whether those solutions actually worked.

Consider the full lifecycle:

1. **MaxDiff** tells you that "accumulate rewards through repeat purchases" is the top unmet need.
2. **Key Driver Analysis** confirms that rewards-related satisfaction is a strong predictor of overall loyalty.
3. Your team designs and ships a loyalty rewards program.
4. **HEART metrics** track whether adoption of the program increases (Adoption), whether users engage with it regularly (Engagement), whether it improves satisfaction scores (Happiness), and whether users with the program churn less (Retention).

Without step 4, you never know if your research-informed decision actually delivered value. You've identified the need, validated it, built a solution — but you have no feedback loop. HEART provides that feedback loop.

**An honest framing:** HEART is not a precision instrument. As Rodden emphasizes, UX metrics are only _proxies_ for user experiences. No metric of user engagement can actually identify how truly engaged a user is, or whether that engagement represents a positive experience. Time spent in a product is often used as a default engagement metric, but high time-spent might reflect confusion, not delight. And optimizing for a single metric can render it useless for evaluation as highlighted by Goodhart's Law ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure").

HEART works best when teams select only the dimensions most relevant to their product, combine quantitative signals with qualitative research to understand the "why" behind metric movements, and are willing to iterate on their metrics as they learn more about what constitutes a positive user experience. Not all five dimensions will be relevant to every product or project.

This honest framing matters because HEART, like any framework, can be misapplied. A team that mechanically tracks all five HEART dimensions without thinking critically about which ones matter will generate dashboard noise, not insight. The value is in the _discipline_ of defining what success looks like before you ship, identifying how you'll measure it, and then actually checking whether it happened.

### Putting the Toolkit Together

The methods in this chapter form a coherent research lifecycle, not a menu of interchangeable alternatives:

- **MaxDiff** answers: _Which needs should we prioritize?_ (point-in-time survey, forced trade-offs)
- **Key Driver Analysis** answers: _Which needs actually drive business outcomes?_ (regression-based behavioral validation)
- **TURF** answers: _Which combination of needs covers the most customers?_ (roadmap optimization)
- **Conjoint** answers: _What bundle of solutions should we build, and at what price?_ (solution design and pricing)
- **HEART + GSM** answers: _Are our solutions actually improving the experience?_ (ongoing behavioral and attitudinal tracking)
- **Constant Sum** answers: _What do customers prioritize?_ (budget-friendly alternative when MaxDiff isn't available)

Not every team needs every method. A startup in early discovery might use only interviews or MaxDiff (combined framing) and supplement with behavioral observation. A mature enterprise product team might use the full chain from MaxDiff through HEART tracking. The point is to match your methods to your questions and your stage. Not to adopt a single methodology because someone said it works.

## Chapter 8 Conclusion

Figuring out what customers truly want is essential to building great products, and the method you use to get those answers matters more than most teams realize. A flawed method can lead to confident decisions based on unreliable data.

MaxDiff provides a more reliable, more practical, and less burdensome alternative to the traditional ODI opportunity algorithm for prioritizing customer needs. By forcing trade-offs rather than collecting inflated ratings, it produces a clearer hierarchy of priorities with fewer respondents and less survey fatigue.

But MaxDiff has its own limitations, and this chapter has tried to be honest about them. It produces relative rankings, not absolute thresholds. It inherits the table-stakes blind spot when framed around unmet needs. It reduces many biases but doesn't eliminate social desirability. And like any survey method, it captures stated preferences (which are not always the same as revealed behavior).

This is why quantification works best as a toolkit, not a single tool. MaxDiff identifies where to focus. Key Driver Analysis validates what actually drives outcomes. HEART tracks whether your solutions deliver. Each method answers a different question, and together they create the feedback loop that the original ODI methodology never provided.

The next chapter explores how to take your prioritized list of needs and translate it into actionable product strategy. Because the hardest part isn't identifying the right needs. It's figuring out what to build.

## Chapter 8 Summary

- The traditional **opportunity algorithm** from Chapter 7 is methodologically flawed. **Maximum Difference Scaling (MaxDiff)** is a statistically sound and practical alternative for prioritizing customer needs.
- MaxDiff works by showing respondents small sets of items and asking them to choose the **most** and **least** important. This forces realistic trade-offs and produces a true hierarchy of needs, eliminating acquiescence bias and rating scale inconsistencies.
- **MaxDiff has limitations.** It produces relative scores (not absolute), inherits the table-stakes blind spot when framed around unmet needs, and doesn't eliminate social desirability bias. These limitations are best addressed through complementary methods, not by abandoning MaxDiff.
- The **measurement decision** matters: combined framing ("which unmet need would make the biggest difference?") works for most teams, while mature products may benefit from importance + targeted satisfaction, and large need lists may call for Relevant Items MaxDiff. **Anchored MaxDiff** adds absolute thresholds when needed.
- **Interpret results as tiers, not strict rankings.** Use confidence intervals to identify meaningful groupings. Watch for table-stakes items that rank low because they're well-served, not unimportant. Cross-reference surprising findings with behavioral data.
- **Key Driver Analysis (KDA)** complements MaxDiff by revealing which needs actually drive business outcomes through regression, solving the stated-vs-derived importance problem and identifying table-stakes needs from behavioral data.
- **The HEART framework** (Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, Task Success) provides the ongoing measurement infrastructure to track whether your solutions actually improve the customer experience — closing the feedback loop that point-in-time research methods cannot.
- **Additional complementary methods** include Constant Sum (budget-friendly forced trade-offs for small-scale studies), TURF Analysis (optimizing which combination of needs to address for maximum customer reach), and Conjoint Analysis (testing solution bundles and pricing after needs are identified).
- **No single method answers every question.** MaxDiff identifies priorities, KDA validates what drives behavior, HEART tracks outcomes. Match your methods to your questions and your product's stage.

**1. Choose Your Own Scenario & Plan Your Study:** Think of a product, service, or experience you're familiar with and would like to improve. For inspiration, you could focus on prioritizing new features for a fitness app, improving the online checkout process for a retail website, understanding what remote employees value most in a work-from-home setup, or enhancing the visitor experience at a local museum.

Once you've chosen your topic, create a brief research plan that defines your single most important research objective, a final list of statements you will test, your chosen design parameters (sample size, items per set, sets per respondent), and the introductory text and instructions for your respondents.

**2. Select a Platform and Build the Survey:** Many survey platforms offer MaxDiff question types. Find one you can access — many offer free trials or have free plans with limited features. Common platforms include Qualtrics, Sawtooth Software, and Conjointly. Input your statements and set up the MaxDiff exercise according to the design parameters you defined in step 1.

**3. Pilot Test Your Survey:** Once your survey is built, don't launch it to hundreds of people. Instead, run a pilot test. Find 2–3 friends or colleagues and send them the survey link. Ask them for feedback: Was anything confusing? Did any of the choices feel impossible to make? How long did it take them? Use their feedback to refine your statements or instructions before a full launch.
