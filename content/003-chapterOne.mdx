The Jobs to be Done (JTBD) framework is built on a simple idea: customers don't buy products, they "hire" them to make progress in their lives. While this concept was formalized in the late 20th century, its roots stretch back much further than most practitioners realize.

## **The Prehistory of an Idea**

Long before JTBD had a name, in 1954, Peter Drucker published _The Practice of Management_, a landmark work that emphasized customer focus and the importance of understanding what value customers actually derive from products rather than what companies think they are selling. [1]

Then came the quote that would be used in all JTBD literature. In 1960, Harvard Business School professor Theodore Levitt published his article "Marketing Myopia" in the Harvard Business Review, arguing that businesses need to shift their focus from producing and selling goods to understanding and meeting customer needs.[2, 3] Levitt used to tell his students, "People don't want a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch hole!" This captured the essence of JTBD before the term existed, establishing the foundation that the customer's desired outcome is the true object of their desire.

> Author's Note: The drill-and-hole insight may predate Levitt. Some historians attribute a similar observation to advertising executive Leo McGivena as early as 1947. Levitt, however, is the canonical source in the JTBD lineage and the one who embedded the concept into the business school curriculum.

It is also worth noting that the human-computer interaction (HCI) and user experience (UX) communities were arriving at similar conclusions through a different lineage entirely. In 1988, Don Norman published _The Design of Everyday Things_, in which he wrote at length about the importance of considering a user's true goal rather than focusing on incremental product improvements. Norman emphasized "root cause analysis" and understanding what the customer is really trying to accomplish—language that echoes the core of JTBD, though Norman never used that term. [21] This parallel history matters because it means JTBD did not emerge in a vacuum. The insight that "understand the goal, not the product" was **converging across multiple disciplines simultaneously**.

## **The Genesis of a Theory: From Engineering Failure to a Formal Process**

The practical history of Jobs to be Done begins with Tony Ulwick. In 1983, Ulwick was an engineer on the IBM PCjr team, building what many expected to become the definitive home computer. The Wall Street Journal had other ideas, declaring it a flop the day after launch. The failure cost IBM over a billion dollars.

That high-profile failure helped shape Ulwick's career. Over the remainder of the 1980s, he studied and tested tools like voice of the customer, quality function deployment (QFD), TRIZ, Six Sigma, and conjoint analysis. By 1990, he had developed a key insight: instead of studying products, study the underlying process customers are trying to complete. He applied Six Sigma and process control thinking to innovation itself, creating a methodology that could identify exactly where customer needs were going unmet.

Ulwick left IBM in 1991 to found The Total Quality Group, now known as Strategyn. His approach, originally called CD-MAP, evolved into Outcome-Driven Innovation.

The methodology's first real test came in 1992 with Cordis Corporation, a medical device company struggling with its angioplasty balloon line. Ulwick's team focused on understanding what cardiologists were actually trying to accomplish when treating blocked arteries, then identified which outcomes mattered most but remained poorly served. The results were dramatic: Cordis went from a negligible market position to over 20% market share and eventually brought the first coronary stent to market. [4]

> Authors Note: the Cordis story is compelling, but it comes almost entirely from Strategyn's own marketing materials, and we should read it with that context in mind. Market share gains of that magnitude are never the result of a single methodology. Regulatory timing, competitive missteps, sales execution, distribution relationships, and the broader trajectory of interventional cardiology in the early 1990s all played a role. ODI may well have been the supporting resource that helped Cordis ask better questions and prioritize the right outcomes, but attributing a leap from obscurity to market leadership solely to one consulting engagement oversimplifies how markets actually work. I include the Cordis case here because it is the origin story of ODI and the proof point Ulwick built his career on, not because I want you to believe that a framework alone can deliver those results. Throughout this book, I will try to be honest about what ODI can and cannot do. It is a unique perspective, not a magic wand.

Around 1999, the company became Strategyn and the process was formally named Outcome-Driven Innovation. That same year, Ulwick introduced ODI and his research and segmentation techniques to Harvard Business School Professor Clayton Christensen in a series of meetings in Cambridge. [4, 5]

One of the highlights of Ulwick's career came in 2002, when Harvard Business Review published an article he wrote called "Turn Customer Input into Innovation," which described ODI and its successful application at Cordis. HBR later named it one of the best business ideas of that year. [5]

## **The Theorist Enters the Picture**

Christensen immediately recognized JTBD as the missing "demand-side" explanation for why customers choose to adopt new solutions. In his 2003 book _The Innovator's Solution_, co-authored with Michael Raynor, Christensen referenced Ulwick's work and used phrases like "circumstances-based categorization." [6] But the full "Jobs to be Done" terminology wouldn't become mainstream until later.

A pivotal moment came in December 2005, when Christensen, Scott Cook, and Taddy Hall published "Marketing Malpractice: The Cause and the Cure" in the Harvard Business Review. [14] This article was the first major publication where Christensen explicitly used the "hire" language that would become the signature vocabulary of JTBD. Christensen and his co-authors argued that the **fundamental unit of analysis** for marketers should not be the customer or the product, but the job the customer is trying to get done. The article also contained an early version of the milkshake story, which Christensen would continue to refine in subsequent publications. This was a turning point: while _The Innovator's Solution_ had introduced the concept academically, "Marketing Malpractice" gave practitioners a concrete, quotable framework to take back to their teams.

That same year, Ulwick published his first full-length book, _What Customers Want: Using Outcome-Driven Innovation to Create Breakthrough Products and Services_ (McGraw-Hill, 2005). [15] This was the first book-length treatment of ODI, detailing an eight-step approach for applying outcome-driven thinking to every aspect of innovation, from segmenting markets and identifying opportunities to creating and evaluating breakthrough concepts. Christensen himself endorsed the work, writing that "Ulwick's outcome-driven programs bring discipline and predictability to the often random process of innovation." Philip Kotler went further, calling Ulwick "the Deming of innovation."

The iconic milkshake story received its fullest early treatment in an April 2007 MIT Sloan Management Review article titled "Finding the Right Job for Your Product," co-authored by Christensen, Scott D. Anthony, Gerald Berstell, and Denise Nitterhouse. [7] When a fast-food restaurant resolved to improve sales of its milkshake, its marketers first defined the market segment by product and then segmented it further by profiling the customer most likely to buy a milkshake. The consequent improvements to the product had no impact on sales.

A new researcher spent a day in a restaurant documenting when each milkshake was bought. He was surprised to find that 40% of all milkshakes were purchased in the early morning. These early-morning customers almost always were alone, they did not buy anything else, and they consumed the milkshakes in their cars. When the researcher returned to interview these morning customers and essentially asked what job they were hiring the milkshake to do, most of them bought their shakes for similar reasons: they faced a long, boring commute and needed something to keep that extra hand busy and to make the commute more interesting. They wanted to consume something that would stave off hunger until noon. [7]

This illustrated that the customer's circumstance and context, not their demographic, is the key to understanding their motivation.

## **The Great Divide: Two Schools of JTBD Thought**

Christensen's popularization of the idea also led to a philosophical split, resulting in two distinct schools of thought that persist today. Understanding this divide is important context to how different practitioners and philosophies go about applying JTBD.

**Jobs-as-Activities (The Ulwick School):** This school defines a "job" as a functional task or activity a person is trying to accomplish. The focus is on the _process_ of getting the job done. Innovation comes from identifying the metrics customers use to measure success (the "desired outcomes") and helping them execute the job better: faster, more predictably, and with greater efficiency. This approach is highly analytical and quantitative, treating innovation like an engineering discipline. ODI is its primary methodology.

"The Customer-Centered Innovation Map" by Lance A. Bettencourt and Anthony W. Ulwick appeared in the May 2008 issue of Harvard Business Review.[8] The article introduced job mapping, a methodology that helps companies analyze the biggest drawbacks of the products and services customers currently use and discover opportunities for innovation. It involves breaking down the task the customer wants to accomplish into eight universal steps. This framework serves as a bridge that many practitioners use regardless of which school they lean toward philosophically.

**Jobs-as-Progress (The Christensen/Moesta School):** This school defines a "job" as the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. It's not about the task itself, but about resolving a struggle and transitioning to a better state. The focus here is on the _why_ behind a customer's decision to change. This perspective explicitly includes the emotional and social dimensions of a decision. Its research methods are qualitative and narrative-based.

Bob Moesta is the president and CEO of the Re-Wired Group, and one of the core figures in the Jobs-To-Be-Done methodology. Moesta's path to JTBD was shaped by an unusual apprenticeship: at age 18, in the mid-1980s, he met and began working with Dr. W. Edwards Deming, the father of the quality revolution. Deming took Moesta to Japan and taught him manufacturing and development methods—an experience that gave Moesta a quality-management foundation that parallels, but is distinct from, Ulwick's Six Sigma roots. [22] This grounding in Deming's systems thinking would later inform how Moesta approached the customer's decision-making process.

Through the 1990s and into the early 2000s, Moesta collaborated extensively with Christensen at Harvard Business School, where together they refined the demand-side perspective on innovation. Moesta and his team at the Re-Wired Group developed frameworks including "The Forces of Progress," which examines the push of the current situation, the pull of the new solution, the anxiety of the new, and the habit force that causes people to resist change.[9] If the push and pull are not greater than the anxiety and habit, people will never switch. The Re-Wired Group also formalized switch interviews, a method of interviewing people who have recently made a purchase to understand how they actually overcame the forces that might have prevented them from switching.

**The Purist: Alan Klement and the Rejection of "Tasks"**

While Moesta and Christensen focused on the "Switch," author Alan Klement took the Jobs-as-Progress philosophy a step further, becoming the vocal critic of the activity-based approach. In his book _When Coffee and Kale Compete_ (2016), Klement argued that viewing a Job as a process or a workflow is a fundamental error, essentially forcing "Task Analysis" into a JTBD wrapper. [19]

Klement defines a Job strictly as the desire for self-betterment: "_A Job to be Done is the process a consumer goes through whenever she aims to change her existing life-situation into a preferred one, but cannot because there are constraints that stop her._" [18]

For Klement, a Job has no functional steps. The "Job" is not to "drill a hole" (an activity); the Job is the emotional struggle to feel proud of one's home. He argues that activities change constantly as technology evolves, but the human desire for progress is the only constant. This perspective created considerable friction in the JTBD community, drawing a hard line between those who view JTBD as an engineering discipline (Ulwick) and those who view it as a psychological investigation (Klement/Moesta). [20]

It's worth noting that this divide also drew criticism from outside the JTBD community. Prominent UX practitioners argued that JTBD was repackaging ideas that the design and usability world had been practicing for decades. Jared Spool, author of _Website Usability_ (1997), went so far as to call JTBD "over-simplified task analysis re-invented by business professors" and "a gimmick to get people who have ignored their users to pay attention to them." [21] Whether one agrees with that characterization or not, the critique highlights an important reality: JTBD exists within a broader ecosystem of user-centered and human-centered design thinking that predates it, and a well-rounded practitioner should understand those adjacent traditions.

## **JTBD Reaches the Mainstream**

In 2016, two publications arrived almost simultaneously. Christensen, Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon, and David S. Duncan released _Competing Against Luck: The Story of Innovation and Customer Choice_.[10] After years of research, the authors came to one important conclusion: the long-held maxim that the crux of innovation is knowing more and more about the customer is wrong. Customers don't simply buy products or services; they hire them to do a job. This book finally brought "Jobs to be Done" into mainstream management literature, though it draws almost entirely from the Jobs-as-Progress school and focuses less on Ulwick's ODI methodology.

Alongside the book, Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan also published a companion article in the Harvard Business Review titled "Know Your Customers' 'Jobs to Be Done'" (September 2016). [16] For many practitioners, this article served as the accessible entry point. The article was much shorter than the book but dense with practical insight. It became one of HBR's most shared articles on the topic and helped crystallize the "Jobs" vocabulary for a corporate audience.

That same year, Ulwick published his second major book, _Jobs to Be Done: Theory to Practice_ (2016), which detailed the refined ODI process and included six in-depth case studies from companies including Microsoft, Bosch, and Arm & Hammer. [23] An independent study conducted with Strategyn's clients found an 86% success rate for innovation projects that used ODI—a five-fold improvement over the industry average. Where _What Customers Want_ (2005) had introduced the methodology, _Theory to Practice_ represented a decade of refinement and included the steps of the full ODI process.

> Author's Note: It is worth noting that this "independent study" was commissioned and funded by Strategyn itself in 2010. The researcher evaluated just 21 product launches from Strategyn's own client base, and success was self-reported by those clients. The 17% industry average it was compared against was aggregated from separate, unrelated sources. This is not the same as a peer-reviewed, third-party audit with controlled conditions. ODI may indeed outperform conventional approaches. However, readers should understand the provenance of this statistic before treating it as fact.

The "Jobs to Be Done" approach can be seen in some of the world's most popular companies including Amazon, Intuit, Uber, Airbnb, Coinbase, Slack, formerly Twitter (now X), and Chobani yogurt.

## **The Spread of JTBD in Tech and Startups**

Corporate adoption followed a predictable pattern. Early adopters from the late 1990s through 2005 were mostly B2B and industrial companies like Cordis, Bosch, and Microsoft. Between 2007 and 2012, B2C and tech companies began adopting the framework.

The explosion came after 2015, particularly in startup and Lean circles. Des Traynor, co-founder at Intercom, helped shine a spotlight on Jobs to be Done when it wasn't yet popular in the tech startup scene. The interviews and content Intercom produced became some of their most popular material. Intercom's book _Intercom on Jobs-to-be-Done_ combined ReWired's foundational concepts alongside fresh thoughts from Intercom employees and affiliates, largely reformatting their popular blog posts into an accessible ebook format. [11]

Another key catalyst in the startup world was Jason Fried and the team at Basecamp (formerly 37signals). Basecamp worked directly with Moesta and Chris Spiek of the Re-Wired Group to apply JTBD to understanding why people "hired" Basecamp. These interviews were fundamentally different from how Basecamp had previously talked to customers, and the resulting insights reshaped their product marketing. The Switch Workshops held at Basecamp's Chicago headquarters helped seed a growing practitioner community, and the famous "mattress interview" conducted at one of those workshops was later featured in Christensen's _Competing Against Luck_. [24]

Another influence in the rise of JTBD in the startup scene is thanks to Ash Maurya, founder of LeanStack and creator of the Lean Business Model Canvas. Ash popularized the operationalization of the "Jobs-as-Progress" philosophy specifically for achieving Need-Solution Fit. [12, 13]

While Moesta and Christensen provided the theoretical foundation for the "Forces of Progress" (Push, Pull, Inertia, Friction), Maurya focused on "simplifying complexity" to make these concepts usable for agile teams. He translated the abstract interplay of forces into a visual "Hill-Climbing" metaphor. In this model, the customer is visualized at the bottom of a hill, needing enough "Push" and "Pull" to overcome the gravity of "Inertia" and "Friction" to reach the top. [12]

Maurya initially captured these insights in the Customer Forces Canvas. However, realizing that static canvases often "collapsed the timeline" and lost the nuance of the customer's journey, he evolved the methodology to focus on narrative structure. Drawing inspiration from Pixar's storytelling rules, Maurya introduced the Customer Forces Story, a "Mad-lib" style framework. This 3-act structure (Inciting Incident → Progressive Complication → Resolution) provides strict guardrails that help teams effectively translate interview notes into a coherent "Global Story" of the customer's journey, greatly increasing the success rate of capturing actionable insights. [12]

## **The Loss of a Champion and the Next Generation**

On January 23, 2020, Clayton Christensen died at age 67 from complications of leukemia. [25] His passing marked the end of an era for the JTBD community, but it did not slow the framework's momentum. If anything, the years surrounding and following Christensen's death saw a new wave of JTBD publications that expanded the framework's reach into new domains.

In 2020, Bob Moesta and Greg Engle published _Demand-Side Sales 101_, which extended JTBD thinking directly into sales methodology. [26] Where earlier JTBD work had focused primarily on product innovation and marketing strategy, _Demand-Side Sales_ argued that the same Forces of Progress and switch interview techniques could transform how salespeople understand and serve buyers. The book drew a sharp distinction between "supply-side" selling (pushing features and benefits) and "demand-side" selling (understanding the customer's struggle and helping them make progress). This represented a meaningful expansion of JTBD beyond its traditional product-development home.

Also in 2020, Jim Kalbach published _The Jobs to Be Done Playbook_, a practitioner-focused guide that attempted something many in the community had been calling for: bridging the two schools of thought into a single, actionable toolkit. [27] Kalbach, then Head of Customer Experience at MURAL, drew from both Ulwick's ODI methodology and Moesta's switch interview approach, presenting them not as competing philosophies but as complementary tools suited to different questions. Both Ulwick and Moesta endorsed the book. For practitioners overwhelmed by the sectarian nature of the JTBD discourse, the Playbook offered a pragmatic middle path.

## **JTBD Today**

The JTBD framework continues to evolve, particularly as organizations grapple with artificial intelligence and the rapid pace of technological change. The core insight, that jobs are stable even as the solutions hired to do them change constantly, has proven especially durable in an era where AI tools are displacing established workflows almost overnight. Practitioners are now applying JTBD thinking not only to product development and sales, but to AI strategy, asking what jobs teams and customers are hiring AI to do, and where those jobs remain underserved.

The community itself has matured. What once existed primarily in books, HBR articles, and conference workshops now lives in online courses, certification programs, dedicated podcasts, Slack communities, and a growing body of academic research. Companies like Strategyn continue to refine the ODI methodology, while practitioners from the Moesta school run workshops and consulting engagements worldwide. The two schools of thought still exist, and the philosophical tension between them remains productive rather than resolved. Which, for a framework built on understanding human struggle, seems fitting.

## **This Book's Focus**

While both schools of thought offer value, this book will focus primarily on the Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI) approach.

I chose this focus not because it is the simplest path, but because it is the most _complex_. Having spent years applying this methodology within a global strategic insights team, I have seen firsthand that ODI is not a "quick fix." In fact, compared to the refreshing, intuitive immediacy of the Moesta, Klement, and Maurya approaches, which are excellent for uncovering the narrative _why_, ODI can feel dauntingly mechanical.

ODI's structured approach is often a double-edged sword. Some are drawn to it as a "silver bullet," hoping its complexity guarantees success. Others are repelled by that same complexity, fearing it will bog down their teams in bureaucracy and data.

The truth lies somewhere in the middle, and that is what we will explore. We are going to peel back the layers of ODI, acknowledging both the clarity it provides and the friction it can create. My goal is to take the complexity that I have seen teams struggle with and break it down into a navigable, step-by-step process.

We will not ignore the other schools of thought. The emotional "push and pull" of the Jobs-as-Progress school provides context that data alone cannot. However, by understanding the _most complex_ approach to JTBD, you will be able to choose whichever implementation you see fit for your own efforts.

In the next chapter, we'll begin our deep dive into the five core steps of the ODI process, looking at exactly how to make this engine work for you.

> There is **no** single right approach to JTBD. It all depends on the level of resolution your team needs. Think of the Jobs-as-Progress (Moesta/Klement) approach as a **Telescope**. It allows you to see the big picture: the market forces, the emotional trajectory of the customer, and the "why" behind the switch. It is perfect for positioning, marketing strategy, and understanding demand. Think of the Jobs-as-Activities (Ulwick/ODI) approach as a **Microscope**. It allows you to zoom in on the specific workflow to see the cracks, the friction, and the inefficiencies invisible to the naked eye. It is perfect for product roadmapping, feature prioritization, and engineering specifications. You do not have to choose between a telescope and a microscope. A good researcher uses both or _other_ research methods.

## **Key Takeaways**

- **The intellectual foundation of JTBD predates its formal name by decades.** Motivation researchers in the 1940s and 1950s, management thinkers like Peter Drucker, marketing pioneers like Theodore Levitt, and design thinkers like Don Norman were all circling the same insight: customers buy outcomes, not products. The UX and HCI communities were arriving at similar conclusions through their own lineage.
- **Tony Ulwick transformed this insight into a rigorous methodology.** His experience with the IBM PCjr failure in 1983, combined with years studying QFD, TRIZ, Six Sigma, and process control thinking, led him to develop what would become Outcome-Driven Innovation. The 1992 Cordis Corporation project provided the first major proof point, with market share jumping to over 20%. His 2002 HBR article and 2005 book _What Customers Want_ established ODI as a discipline.
- **Clayton Christensen popularized the "Jobs to be Done" terminology but did not invent the underlying concept.** He repeatedly credited Ulwick with originating the functional job framework, while his own contribution was adding emotional and social dimensions. His 2005 HBR article "Marketing Malpractice" introduced the "hire" language, the famous milkshake study appeared in a 2007 MIT Sloan Management Review article, and his 2016 book _Competing Against Luck_ brought JTBD to mainstream audiences. Christensen passed away on January 23, 2020.
- **Two distinct schools of thought—and occasionally conflict—exist today.** The **Jobs-as-Activities** school (Ulwick) treats innovation as an engineering discipline, focusing on functional steps and metrics. The **Jobs-as-Progress** school (Christensen/Moesta) focuses on the emotional "switch" and self-betterment. **Author Alan Klement further polarized this divide by arguing that "Jobs have no functional steps," strictly defining a Job as the desire to change one's life situation.** Critics from the UX community have pointed out that both schools share significant overlap with pre-existing task analysis and user-centered design practices.
- **There is no single "right" approach; it depends on the question you need to answer.** The Progress school helps answer _why_ a customer buys (ideal for Marketing & Sales), while the Activities school answers _what_ to build to satisfy them (ideal for Product & R&D).
- **JTBD adoption has followed a pattern from industrial B2B to mainstream SaaS.** Early adopters in the late 1990s and early 2000s were companies like Cordis, Bosch, and Microsoft. The framework reached mainstream startup culture after 2015, driven largely by content from companies like Intercom and Basecamp, and practitioners in the Lean Startup movement. Post-2020 publications by Moesta (_Demand-Side Sales 101_) and Kalbach (_The Jobs to Be Done Playbook_) have expanded JTBD into sales methodology and offered practitioner-friendly bridges between the two schools.
