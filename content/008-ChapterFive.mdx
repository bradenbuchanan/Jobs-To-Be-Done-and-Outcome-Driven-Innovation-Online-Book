With the core job defined, the next step is deconstructing it. The Job Map helps you understand the different steps the main job executor takes in order to get the job done.

## What Is a Job Map?

<BookImage
  src="/images/book/chapter-05/jobmapillustration.png"
  alt="Generic Job Map Template"
  caption="Generic Job Map Template"
  width={600}
  height={234}
  standalone={true}
/>

After you have determined the core functional job that will serve as the foundation for your JTBD research, the next part of step two in the ODI process is to build out the job map. The Job Map breaks the core functional job into a series of solution-agnostic steps that represent the ideal path to success. I like to think of it as a solution-agnostic customer journey map, though the two tools differ in important ways that we will explore shortly.

Tony Ulwick says, "A job map does not show what the customer is doing (a solution view); rather, it describes what the customer is trying to get done (a needs view)." [30]

That distinction is the heartbeat of the entire exercise. The job map is not interested in which buttons a customer clicks, which app they open, or which vendor they call. It is interested in the goals they are trying to achieve at each stage of getting the job done. A well-constructed job map follows a set of key principles:

- **Solution-agnostic:** The steps describe what needs to happen, not how it currently happens with a particular product or service.
- **Generalized:** The job steps must be relevant to anyone doing the job, regardless of their specific tools or context.
- **Ideal:** All job steps should be placed in the optimal order for execution.
- **Functional:** All job steps of the core job must be functional, not emotional. (Emotional and social dimensions are captured later in the process as related jobs and desired outcomes.)
- **Properly formatted:** Each step follows the same syntax rules we established for core functional jobs: verb + object + contextual clarifier, free of jargon or solution-specific language.
- **Active:** Each step states what the customer is trying to accomplish, not what is happening to them.
- **Complete:** The job map should cover the full scope of the job from beginning to end. Most teams land between 8 and 15 steps, though complex domains like manufacturing, medicine, or enterprise procurement can push beyond that range.

### Job Map vs. Customer Journey Map

<BookImage
  src="/images/book/chapter-05/jobmapVsCustomerJourneyMap.png"
  alt="Difference between job map and customer journey map"
  caption="Difference Between a Job Map and Customer Journey Map"
  width={600}
  height={327}
  standalone={true}
/>

The distinction between Job Maps and customer journey maps reflects a fundamental difference in perspective and purpose, and understanding it is important before you start building.

Customer journey maps focus on documenting the current experience customers have with a specific company, product, or service. They map touchpoints, emotions, and pain points within the **existing** relationship. This makes them valuable for improving current customer experience, but it also limits their scope to incremental improvements within the current solution paradigm.

Job Maps start from a different place entirely. They focus on the job itself rather than any particular solution. This job-centric perspective reveals the complete process customers need to execute, including steps that current solutions may not address at all or may address poorly. By maintaining solution agnosticism, Job Maps expose white-space opportunities and highlight fundamental job steps that competitors may be ignoring or underserving.

The scope also differs between these two tools. Customer journey maps typically capture the experience from initial awareness through purchase and ongoing use of a specific solution, reflecting the progression through a company's sales and service processes. Job Maps focus on the execution timeframe of the core functional job itself, which may be much shorter or longer than any individual customer journey and may involve multiple solutions or no solutions at all.

This difference in scope and perspective makes Job Maps the stronger tool for identifying strategic opportunities, while customer journey maps excel at optimizing existing customer relationships and experiences. Organizations benefit most from using both in complementary ways, with Job Maps informing strategic decisions and customer journey maps guiding tactical experience improvements.

**A word of caution:** In practice, many teams already have customer journey maps and attempt to reverse-engineer a job map from them. This is a common mistake. Because the journey map is built around a specific product's touchpoints, its solution-specific framing tends to contaminate the job map. Steps like "Browse the product catalog" or "Contact support" are journey map steps, not job map steps. If you have an existing journey map, it can serve as useful background context, but you should build your job map independently from first principles rather than translating one format into the other.

## How to Build a Job Map

### Step 1: Starting with Your Core Functional Job

See [Chapter 4](https://claude.ai/book/007-chapterFour) for help determining the core functional job of your product or service. Once you have that, you can start building out your job map.

To provide a working example throughout this chapter, we will use the core job: "Communicate information clearly to an audience for understanding."

### Step 2: Identifying the Job Steps

<BookImage
  src="/images/book/chapter-05/jobmapillustration.png"
  alt="Generic Job Map Template"
  caption="Generic Job Map Template"
  width={600}
  height={234}
  standalone={true}

The next step is to identify the job steps for a core functional job. The recommended starting point is the 8-step job map template, originally introduced by Bettencourt and Ulwick in their 2008 Harvard Business Review article "The Customer-Centered Innovation Map." [28]

These eight steps provide a universal structure that applies across nearly any functional job. Think of them as guardrails that ensure you do not miss a major phase of the job. Your final map will often expand beyond eight steps as you discover additional complexity, but starting here keeps you grounded.

The 8 steps are listed below, along with questions you can ask participants during interviews:

1. **Define**

- Where/when does the job begin? The job begins when there is a need to share specific information with others for a purpose.
- What must be defined up front? The communication objective, target audience characteristics, key message, desired outcome, and success criteria.

2. **Locate**

- What must be located, gathered, or retrieved? Relevant data, supporting evidence, credible sources, audience contact information, appropriate communication channels, and any existing materials or templates.

3. **Prepare**

- What must be organized, examined, or set up? Structure the information logically, choose the right format or medium, prepare visual aids if needed, schedule timing, and set up the delivery environment or platform.

4. **Confirm**

- What must be validated before execution? Verify information accuracy, confirm audience availability, test technical setup, review message clarity, and ensure all materials are ready.

5. **Execute**

- What is at the heart of getting the job done? Deliver the core message using the chosen communication method, engage the audience appropriately, and present information in a clear, structured manner.

> **Author's Note**: The Execute step is not the entire job. It is the moment the user engages in the core action. For example, in "dental surgery," the job includes preparation and recovery, but the Execute step is the surgery itself.

6. **Monitor**

- What must be monitored during execution? Audience comprehension signals (questions, body language, engagement), technical performance, time management, and message reception.

7. **Modify**

- What adjustments might be needed? Clarify confusing points, adjust pace or complexity, address unexpected questions, resolve technical issues, or adapt to audience feedback in real time.

8. **Conclude**

- What must be done to successfully conclude? Summarize key points, confirm understanding, provide follow-up resources, establish next steps, and document outcomes or lessons learned.

These eight steps are the recommended starting point. From here you can branch into more granular or less granular steps depending on how detailed your job map needs to be.

### Step 3: Conducting the Interview

Now let's walk through what a job map interview actually looks like. Imagine we are using our core functional job of "Communicate information clearly to an audience for understanding." Below is a transcript showing how the questions for each job map step can be asked and how the responses map to the framework.

**Example Interview**

**Interviewer:** Let's start with the heart of what you are trying to accomplish. When you need to communicate important information to your team, what is the most central task, the core of what you are trying to get done?

**Marketing Manager:** Well, it is really about **delivering the message in a way they can act on it** [Execute]. I am usually presenting findings from our customer research or explaining a new campaign strategy.

**Interviewer:** Got it. Now let's back up. Where does this job actually begin for you? What triggers the need to communicate?

> **Author's Note**: Notice that we do not start the conversation by asking about the beginning of the job (the Define step). Instead, we start by asking about the Execute step, the heart of the action. It is often easier for customers to describe the main action first (e.g., "delivering the presentation"). Once they are grounded in that moment, it becomes much easier for them to work backward and answer questions like, "What did you have to plan or define before you reached that point?"

**Marketing Manager:** It usually starts when I have **new insights that will change how the team approaches their work** [Define]. Like last week, our user research revealed customers were confused about our pricing tiers.

**Interviewer:** What do you need to figure out or define before you can move forward?

**Marketing Manager:** I need to be clear on **what action I want them to take afterward** [Define]. Am I asking them to change existing campaigns? Create new materials? Just be aware for future work? That shapes everything else.

**Interviewer:** That is the "Define" step emerging. What do you need to gather or locate to make this work?

**Marketing Manager:** I pull together **the actual research data, maybe some customer quotes, competitive examples** [Locate]. I also need to **check everyone's calendars** [Locate] and figure out if this needs to be email, Slack, or a proper meeting.

**Interviewer:** Before you actually deliver the information, what do you need to prepare or set up?

**Marketing Manager:** I usually **create an outline or slides if it is complex** [Prepare]. For the pricing thing, I made a simple visual showing the customer confusion points. I also **book a conference room** [Prepare] and make sure the projector works. Learned that the hard way!

**Interviewer:** What do you double-check or confirm before you actually start communicating?

**Marketing Manager:** I **rehearse key points to myself** [Confirm], especially if there might be pushback. I also **verify my facts** [Confirm]. Nothing kills credibility like getting basic numbers wrong.

**Interviewer:** During the actual communication, what are you monitoring or watching for?

**Marketing Manager:** **Facial expressions are huge** [Monitor]. I can see when people are confused or skeptical. I watch for **when people start checking phones** [Monitor], because that means I am losing them. I also listen for **the types of questions** [Monitor] they ask.

**Interviewer:** When do you need to modify or adjust your approach?

**Marketing Manager:** If I see confusion, I **slow down and give more examples** [Modify]. If they seem skeptical, I **pull out additional data** [Modify] or ask them to share their concerns. Sometimes I realize mid-presentation that **I should have started with the business impact** [Modify] rather than the research details.

**Interviewer:** How do you know when the job is successfully completed?

**Marketing Manager:** When **people start talking about next steps without me prompting them** [Conclude]. Like, "So should we pause the current email campaign?" or "I can update those landing pages by Friday." That tells me they understood and are ready to act.

**Interviewer:** What do you do to wrap things up?

**Marketing Manager:** I always **send a follow-up email with the key points and any agreed actions** [Conclude]. I also **set calendar reminders to check in** [Conclude] on whether changes actually happened.

### Key Job Steps Revealed:

1. **Define:** Determine desired action/outcome
2. **Locate:** Gather supporting data
3. **Prepare:** Organize setup
4. **Confirm:** Validate delivery
5. **Execute:** Deliver message while engaging audience
6. **Monitor:** Monitor audience engagement
7. **Modify:** Adjust approach based on real-time feedback
8. **Conclude:** Confirm next steps

**Notice how the generic 8-step template guided the questioning, but the job steps emerged naturally from the conversation rather than being imposed.**

### The 8 Steps Are Your Starting Point, Not Your Limit

The 8 generic steps serve as guardrails, not requirements. Depending on your core job's complexity, you might end up with anywhere from 8 to 15 steps. A straightforward consumer task might compress into fewer steps, while complex B2B processes, manufacturing operations, or regulated industries like healthcare often require additional steps to capture the full reality.

Use the 8 steps as your starting framework to ensure you do not miss major phases, then expand where your customer interviews reveal that a single generic step actually contains multiple distinct activities.

#### Taking Your Interview Insights and Creating Order

Let's return to our communication example and expand it beyond the basic 8 steps. After conducting several interviews, you might have gathered insights like these:

- I need to know what action I want them to take
- I have to figure out if this is urgent or can wait
- I check everyone's calendars
- I need to gather the right supporting evidence
- I create different materials for different audiences
- I rehearse key points to myself
- I watch facial expressions during the presentation
- I adjust my approach if people seem confused
- I send a follow-up email afterward
- I check back later to see if action was actually taken

Your first task is to map these insights using the 8 core steps as your foundation, then expand where the complexity demands it:

**Define Phase:**

1. **Assess Urgency** - Determine timing and priority level
2. **Define Outcome** - Clarify desired action and success criteria
3. **Identify Audience** - Understand who needs this information

**Locate Phase:**

4. **Gather Evidence** - Collect supporting data and examples
5. **Check Availability** - Confirm audience schedules and accessibility
6. **Select Channels** - Choose appropriate communication methods

**Prepare Phase:**

7. **Create Materials** - Develop audience-appropriate content
8. **Setup Environment** - Book space and test technology

**Confirm Phase:**

9. **Validate Content** - Verify facts and rehearse delivery

**Execute Phase:**

10. **Deliver Message** - Present information while engaging audience

**Monitor Phase:**

11. **Track Reception** - Watch for comprehension and engagement signals

**Modify Phase:**

12. **Adjust Approach** - Adapt based on real-time feedback

**Conclude Phase:**

13. **Secure Commitment** - Confirm understanding and next steps
14. **Follow Up** - Check that promised actions actually occur

### When Different Executors Follow Different Sequences

One challenge that frequently arises during job mapping is discovering that different job executors follow meaningfully different sequences for the same core job. For example, in our communication job, a CEO preparing a board presentation follows a different process than a marketing manager presenting campaign results to their team. The CEO might spend far more time in the Confirm and Validate phases and far less time in Locate, because their information is being prepared by others.

This raises an important question: does this variation mean you are looking at different jobs, different segments, or simply acceptable variation within one map?

The answer depends on **how fundamental** the differences are. If the core goal is the same (communicating information clearly to an audience for understanding) and the major phases are the same, but the emphasis and time allocation differ, you are likely looking at different segments within the same job. These segments will become important later in the ODI process when you quantify unmet needs and discover that different groups of executors are overserved or underserved at different steps.

However, if you discover that certain executors skip entire phases, or that the fundamental goal shifts (for example, one group is trying to communicate information while another is trying to build consensus around a decision), you may be looking at genuinely different core jobs. In that case, revisit Chapter 4 and consider whether your job definition is too broad.

When in doubt, note the variation and keep going. The quantitative phases of ODI are specifically designed to surface these differences through segmentation. Your job map does not need to resolve every variation upfront. It needs to capture the complete set of steps that any executor might follow, even if not every executor follows all of them.

### Ensuring Complete Coverage

Look for gaps in your expanded framework. If you have detailed steps for preparation but nothing about follow-up, go back to your interviewees and ask: "After the communication is over, what do you do to ensure it was successful?"

Watch for phases that seem too sparse. Complex jobs often have multiple sub-steps within each major phase. A manufacturing process might have 3-4 steps just within the Confirm phase. A legal process might have extensive steps within Define that would be entirely absent from a consumer job.

### Sequencing the Steps Properly

While the 8 generic steps provide a natural flow, pay attention to the actual sequence your customers follow. Sometimes they will say things like:

"Actually, I usually start gathering data before I am even clear on what I want to accomplish."

This suggests their job might actually start with "Gather Evidence" activities that help them "Define Outcome." Do not force their reality into the template. **Adapt the template to reflect how the job really gets done**. The 8-step template describes a logical ideal, but real execution often involves loops, overlaps, and reordering. Your final map should reflect the most common and effective path, while acknowledging that rigid linearity is more of an aspiration than a reality for most jobs.

### Creating a Usable Format

Transform your organized insights into a clear job map format:

**Job:** Communicate information clearly to an audience for understanding

1. **Assess Urgency** - Determine timing and priority level
2. **Define Outcome** - Clarify desired action and success criteria
3. **Identify Audience** - Understand who needs this information
4. **Gather Evidence** - Collect supporting data and examples
5. **Check Availability** - Confirm audience schedules and accessibility
6. **Select Channels** - Choose appropriate communication methods
7. **Create Materials** - Develop audience-appropriate content
8. **Setup Environment** - Book space and test technology
9. **Validate Content** - Verify facts and rehearse delivery
10. **Deliver Message** - Present information while engaging audience
11. **Track Reception** - Watch for comprehension and engagement signals
12. **Adjust Approach** - Adapt based on real-time feedback
13. **Secure Commitment** - Confirm understanding and next steps
14. **Follow Up** - Check that promised actions actually occur

Each step should have a clear trigger (what prompts this step) and outcome (what is accomplished when it is complete). This creates a logical flow that others can follow and validate.

The right number of steps is however many it takes to accurately capture your customers' reality. Simple jobs might compress to 8 steps, complex industrial processes might expand beyond 15. The 8-step framework exists to ensure you do not miss any major phases along the way.

## Identifying the Right Granularity

One of the most common challenges in job mapping is finding the right level of detail. Too granular, and your job map becomes unwieldy and loses strategic value. Too high-level, and you miss the specific pain points where innovation can happen. The key is finding the zone where each step reveals actionable insights without drowning you in micro-actions.

### The Granularity Test: Can You Innovate Here?

Ask yourself whether a product, service, or solution could make this step noticeably better, faster, or easier. If the answer is yes, you are at the right level of granularity. If not, you may need to zoom in or zoom out.

Consider these three attempts at capturing the same moment in our communication job:

"Click the send button" represents too much granularity, because this micro-action offers little opportunity for meaningful innovation. No one is struggling with the act of clicking.

"Communicate the message" is too broad, because it encompasses too much and obscures the specific pain points within the overall activity.

"Deliver message while engaging audience" captures the right level, because it represents a meaningful job step with clear opportunities for improvement through better presentation tools, engagement techniques, and feedback mechanisms.

### Consider Your Scope

The right granularity also depends on what you are trying to innovate. If you are building communication software, you might need more granular steps around content creation and delivery, because those are the areas where your product can make a difference. If you are designing organizational processes as a consultant, you might focus on higher-level coordination steps like aligning stakeholders on messages and cascading information through hierarchy.

From a software company perspective, you might zoom in on steps like creating slides, formatting content for mobile, and tracking message opens, because you can build better tools for those activities. From an organizational consultant perspective, you might emphasize the Define and Conclude phases, because that is where communication breaks down at the process level rather than the tool level.

Neither perspective is wrong. They are optimized for different types of innovation.

### Test with the "So What?" Question

For each step, ask what could go wrong and what would make this noticeably better. If you cannot generate compelling answers to either question, the step might be too granular, too broad, or not truly part of the core job.

A step like "Open the presentation software" fails this test. What could go wrong? Not much. What would make it better? It is already trivial. This is a micro-action, not a job step.

A step like "Create materials that match the audience's level of understanding" passes easily. What could go wrong? You could pitch the content at the wrong level of complexity, losing your audience or insulting their intelligence. What would make it better? Tools that help you assess audience expertise, templates calibrated to different knowledge levels, or AI-assisted content adaptation.

### Practical Granularity Guidelines

Right-sized steps typically share several characteristics. They have a clear beginning and end. They involve a decision or create an output. They can fail or succeed independently. They present opportunities for measurement. And they could benefit from tools, services, or improvements.

Warning signs of wrong granularity include steps that are sub-tasks of other steps, steps with no clear success criteria, steps that never fail or cause problems, steps that require no decisions or judgment, and steps that offer no room for innovation.

Remember that you can always adjust granularity later. Start with what feels natural from your interviews, then refine based on where you see the most potential for improvement and the most customer frustration.

## Validating Your Job Map

A job map is only useful if it reflects reality. Validation is the process of ensuring that the map you have built actually represents how people get the job done in the field, not just how you think they do it.

### Choosing Your Validation Approach

The approach you take to validation depends on your familiarity with the core functional job and the domain expertise required. There are two primary workflows, and it is worth being explicit about when to use each one.

**Workflow 1: Interview-first (for unfamiliar domains).** When you are entering a domain where you lack direct experience, start with open-ended interviews. Use the 8-step template to guide your questioning, but let the job map emerge from what you hear. Do not bring a pre-built hypothesis to these conversations, because your assumptions about how the job works are likely to be wrong in ways you cannot predict. This is the approach demonstrated in our interview example above.

**Workflow 2: Hypothesis-first (for familiar domains).** When you have significant experience with the core job or access to substantial secondary research, start by building a hypothesized job map before talking to users. If you are working in healthcare and evaluating a core job around using medical devices, begin by reading medical reports, research articles, and reviewing transcripts related to that job. This preparation creates a stronger foundation for your validation interviews, because you can ask more targeted questions and focus the conversation on areas of uncertainty rather than covering well-understood ground.

The key difference is this: in the first workflow, interviews generate the map. In the second, interviews validate and refine a map you have already drafted. Both are legitimate approaches. Choose based on how much you already know.

### How Many Interviews Do You Need?

For jobs you have personally experienced many times, you might feel confident validating your job map with 2 to 4 interviews. Our communication example around presenting information to an audience is a good case. If this represents a core job you have executed repeatedly throughout your career, you can often validate the steps based on your own experience combined with minimal external input.

For core jobs that require industry knowledge or domain expertise in fields like manufacturing, medicine, or law, you will typically need 5 to 8 validation interviews. These specialized domains have nuances and steps that only subject matter experts would recognize. A surgeon's "Confirm" phase before an operation involves steps that no amount of secondary research would fully reveal.

### What Does "Validated" Actually Mean?

Validation does not mean that every interviewee describes the exact same sequence in the exact same language. People will naturally describe their process differently, emphasize different phases, and occasionally disagree about ordering. This is expected.

What you are looking for is **convergence** on the major phases. If 4 out of 5 interviewees describe some version of a preparation step, a confirmation step, and a follow-up step, your map is converging even if the specific language varies. If interviewee 3 describes a fundamentally different sequence from interviewees 1 and 2, that is a signal worth investigating. It might mean one of three things: the interviewee is an outlier with an unusual process, the job has legitimate variants that correspond to different segments, or your job definition is too broad and you are actually mapping two different jobs.

When you encounter significant disagreement, the most productive response is to ask directly. "Other people I have spoken with describe doing X before Y. You seem to do it the other way around. Can you help me understand why?" This often reveals contextual factors (team size, industry, urgency) that explain the variation and help you decide whether to accommodate it in a single map or split into separate maps.

### The Validation Interview Process

After developing your hypothesized or interview-derived job map, conduct 3 to 5 follow-up interviews with main job executors. Present your job map and ask targeted questions about its accuracy:

- Are there missing steps they would add?
- Do they perceive any steps as confusing or unnecessary?
- Would they sequence anything differently?
- Are there steps that feel like they belong together and should be combined?
- Are there steps that feel too broad and should be split?

These validation interviews serve a dual purpose. Beyond confirming your job map's accuracy, they provide deeper insights into the different outcomes within each job step and the complexity factors that influence execution. [See Chapter 6 for more details on outcomes and complexity factors.](https://claude.ai/book/009-ChapterSix) This additional layer of understanding matters for identifying the most impactful opportunities downstream.

The goal is not perfection but confidence. You need to be confident that your job map reflects the real experience of people executing this job in the field, not a sanitized or idealized version of it.

## Using the Job Map for Competitive Positioning

Now that you have a validated job map, one of its most useful applications is evaluating where your company's solutions stand relative to the competition. The job map provides a neutral framework for comparison because it is not organized around any single product's features. It is organized around what customers need to accomplish.

To illustrate this, let's look at a crowded market: music streaming. Companies like Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube Music, Tidal, and many more all exist in this space.

Let's imagine we are part of the consumer strategy group looking at how we could expand the core Spotify solution into other opportunities. To get a sense of where things currently are in the market, let's create the job map for Spotify's core functional job: "Listen to Music."

### Elevating to a Higher Flight Level

As we discussed in [Chapter 4](https://claude.ai/book/007-chapterFour) about flight levels, when the current core job shows limited opportunity for differentiation, evaluating a higher-level job can reveal adjacent markets and strategic possibilities. A useful technique for identifying this higher-level job is to revisit a company's core mission statement, which often articulates an aspiration beyond the current product.

Spotify's mission statement reads:

> [Our mission is to unlock the potential of human creativity—by giving a million creative artists the opportunity to live off their art and billions of fans the opportunity to enjoy and be inspired by it.](https://newsroom.spotify.com/company-info/#:~:text=Our%20mission%20is%20to%20unlock%20the%20potential%20of%20human%20creativity%E2%80%94by%20giving%20a%20million%20creative%20artists%20the%20opportunity%20to%20live%20off%20their%20art%20and%20billions%20of%20fans%20the%20opportunity%20to%20enjoy%20and%20be%20inspired%20by%20it.) - Spotify

Focusing on the consumer side of this mission, "billions of fans the opportunity to enjoy and be inspired by it," the key functional elements are "enjoy" and "be inspired by" creative content. If we apply our core functional job syntax to this:

- The verb would be "Discover" (since enjoyment and inspiration require finding the right content)
- The object is the creative content itself
- The contextual clarifier captures the purpose: for personal enjoyment and inspiration

Importantly, notice that Spotify's mission does not limit this to music. It refers to "art" and "creativity" broadly. Given Spotify's expansion into podcasts and audiobooks, we can see they have already recognized this broader opportunity. So our higher flight level core functional job becomes: "Discover audio content for personal enjoyment and inspiration."

This reframing shifts us from thinking about Spotify as a music company competing with Apple Music and YouTube Music to thinking about them as an audio content discovery platform competing in a much larger market that includes educational content, storytelling, news, and any other audio that can entertain or inspire people.

However, as we discussed in Chapter 4, there is a real risk to going too high. "Discover audio content for personal enjoyment and inspiration" is broad enough that it arguably competes with reading books, watching television, attending live events, or even browsing social media. At some point, a job definition becomes so expansive that it loses strategic focus. The right flight level is the one where you can see new markets clearly without losing the ability to make concrete product decisions. For our purposes, "discover audio content" is a productive elevation because it stays within Spotify's core medium (audio) while opening up new content categories.

### Adjacent Market Opportunities

The elevated job map reveals several categories of opportunity.

**Educational audio** represents a substantial market. Millions of people are trying to learn new languages through interactive audio courses or develop professional skills through specialized programs. University students and lifelong learners seek academic lectures and educational content they can consume while commuting or exercising. The children's market presents a distinct opportunity, with parents actively seeking interactive audio stories and educational content.

**Wellness and lifestyle audio** has been growing as more people seek guided meditation, sleep content, and fitness coaching. The increasing attention to mental health has created demand for audio content that supports emotional wellbeing and mindfulness practices.

**Interactive and social audio** represents an emerging category where Spotify could facilitate live audio events, create community features around shared listening experiences, and develop immersive audio experiences that move beyond traditional passive consumption.

**Professional audio** presents opportunities in business-specific content, personalized news curation, and productivity tools that help people stay informed. The shift toward remote work has increased demand for audio-based collaboration and meeting tools.

**Emerging audio technologies** like spatial audio, AI-generated personalized content, and real-time audio translation could position Spotify at the forefront of next-generation audio innovation, creating categories that do not yet have established competition.

### Why the Job Map Makes This Analysis Possible

By viewing these new markets through the job map, Spotify can see that its existing capabilities provide a strong foundation for expansion.

Their recommendation and personalization engines are directly transferable. The algorithms that help users examine content attributes and compare alternatives for music can be applied with equal effectiveness to podcasts, audiobooks, or educational lectures. This core competency gives them an immediate advantage in helping users find the right content regardless of format.

Their expertise in content organization and delivery provides a ready-made infrastructure for new verticals. The logic used to organize music into playlists can be adapted to structure educational courses by skill level or wellness content by specific need. The infrastructure built to support offline listening and cross-device sync is agnostic to content type.

Perhaps most significantly, Spotify's behavioral data reveals patterns about when and how users seek different types of audio throughout their day. This intelligence is particularly valuable for entering markets like wellness, where understanding a user's context and emotional state can determine whether content is helpful or irrelevant.

None of these strategic insights would emerge from a customer journey map anchored to Spotify's current product. They emerge because the job map is organized around what customers need, not what Spotify currently offers.

## Conclusion

The job map is one of the most versatile tools in the ODI toolkit. It provides a structured, solution-agnostic view of how customers get their core functional job done, and that view serves as the foundation for everything that follows in the ODI process.

Here are the key principles to carry forward:

- A Job Map provides a clear framework for deconstructing a core functional job into its essential, solution-agnostic steps. Its focus on the user's underlying goals, rather than their interaction with a specific product, is what distinguishes it from a traditional customer journey map.
- The universal 8-step template serves as a starting point for structuring customer interviews and ensuring comprehensive coverage of the job. It is a set of guardrails, not a rigid structure.
- Effective job mapping requires moving beyond the generic template to add, combine, or resequence steps that accurately reflect the customer's true process. Most teams land between 8 and 15 steps, though complex domains may require more.
- Determining the correct level of granularity for each step is critical. The test is simple: can you innovate here? If a product or service could make this step noticeably better, faster, or easier, you are at the right level.
- When different executors follow different sequences, determine whether you are looking at different segments (same job, different emphasis) or genuinely different jobs (different goals). The quantitative phases of ODI are designed to surface these differences through segmentation.
- Validation means convergence on major phases, not perfect agreement on every detail. Choose your validation approach (interview-first or hypothesis-first) based on your domain familiarity.
- The Job Map is a powerful tool for competitive positioning. By mapping competitors against each job step, you can identify where the market is well-served and where gaps exist.
- Elevating the core functional job to a higher flight level can reveal adjacent market opportunities, but be cautious about going so high that the job loses strategic focus.
