Defining the market around the job-to-be-done is the first step in the ODI process, setting the foundation for all subsequent research efforts.

When using the ODI approach, we use a specific syntax to ensure we are looking at the market correctly:

**Market = [Job Executor] + [The Job-to-be-Done]**

This implies that a "market" is not defined by a region (e.g., "The US Market") or a technology (e.g., "The SaaS Market"). Instead, a market is simply a group of people trying to get a specific job done.

This formula has two halves, and both need to be right. Get the executor wrong and you'll spend months researching the needs of someone who doesn't actually do the work. Get the job wrong and you'll optimize for an outcome nobody cares about. Every downstream phase of ODI such as your outcome statements, your quantitative survey, your segments, and your strategy based on the results will inherit whatever you put into this formula.

This chapter focuses on the first half: identifying _who_ the job executor is. The next chapter will tackle how to define the job itself at the right level of precision.

## Why define the market through a JTBD lens?

Traditional market definitions often revolve around products or solutions, which limits our understanding of customer needs and opportunities.

Consider the evolution of navigation. If we had defined the market as "paper maps," we would have missed the fundamental job that people were trying to get done: "Determine the most time-efficient route for travel." This job has remained constant even as solutions evolved from paper maps to GPS devices to smartphone navigation apps.

The key to defining markets through a JTBD lens is to shift focus from the solution to the underlying job. When companies do this, the competitive landscape changes dramatically.

Take the fitness industry. Rather than defining separate markets for treadmills, fitness classes, and personal training, consider the deeper core job. If someone's core job is "increase longevity," then Peloton, local gyms, and fitness apps aren't just competing with each other — they're competing with everything from meditation apps and dietary supplements to sleep tracking devices and preventive healthcare services. If the core job is "feel confident in my appearance," the competition extends beyond traditional fitness to include clothing brands, cosmetics, mental health apps, and even drugs like GLP-1.

This broader understanding reveals that companies often define their competition too narrowly. A fitness equipment manufacturer focusing solely on competing with other equipment makers might miss that their real competition includes walking groups, gardening clubs, or even social dancing classes. All of which might serve the customer's true job just as effectively.

The transportation industry offers another illustration. Travis Kalanick and the Uber founding team didn't set out to build a "better taxi company." They focused on the job of "getting from point A to point B reliably and conveniently."[22, 23] This perspective helped them see that their competition included not just taxis, but car ownership, public transportation, and even decisions to stay home. Airbnb similarly focused not on "short-term rentals" but on "belonging anywhere,"[24] leading them to compete with the entire travel experience and eventually expand into experiences and long-term stays.

When companies define their markets around jobs rather than products or technologies, they create a stable foundation regardless of how the technology changes. But identifying the right job starts with identifying the right _person_ — the job executor.

## The Job Executor, Product Support Team, and Purchase Decision Maker

One of the key steps in defining your market is identifying who actually executes the core functional job. While this might seem straightforward, many teams stumble by confusing the job executor with buyers or support personnel. It is vital to understand the distinction between three key roles:

- **The Job Executor:** The person using the product to get the job done.
- **The Product Lifecycle Support Team:** The people who install, maintain, or clean the product.
- **The Purchase Decision Maker:** The person who pays for the product.

The **Job Executor** is the person or role that performs the core functional job — the **fundamental reason why your market exists**. This person directly uses your product or service to accomplish their goal.

Consider the healthcare technology market: A hospital administrator might make purchasing decisions, and IT staff might handle installation and maintenance, but if you're developing surgical instruments, your **primary job executor** is the surgeon. Their needs, constraints, and desired outcomes should drive your core product efforts.

**Product Lifecycle Support Teams** play a different role. These individuals handle the **consumption chain jobs** — all the tasks associated with the product's lifecycle, encompassing everything from installation and maintenance to storage and disposal. They impact the overall customer experience but don't execute the core job. In a manufacturing setting, while machine operators are the job executors for production equipment, maintenance technicians form the support team, handling tasks like equipment calibration and repairs.

The **Purchase Decision Maker (or buyer)** holds the purse strings but may have limited interaction with the product itself. In B2B contexts, this role often belongs to procurement teams or senior management. While their needs must be addressed in your go-to-market strategy and business model, their requirements shouldn't drive core product innovation.

> **Author's Note:** Be careful here. In B2B software, it is tempting to build features exclusively for the Purchase Decision Maker (e.g., fancy admin dashboards or compliance reports) because that is what "closes the deal." However, if you neglect the Job Executor, you end up creating "Shelfware" — software that is bought but never used. Long-term retention only comes from satisfying the Job Executor. I've watched teams spend entire quarters building executive reporting dashboards while their actual users churned. The dashboard looked great in the sales demo. Nobody used the product.

The key lies in properly **identifying** and **prioritizing** these roles during your research and development process.

## How to actually find your job executor

The table above makes role identification look clean. In practice, it's messy. The biggest challenge isn't understanding the three roles conceptually, it's figuring out which person in a complex organization or household actually _does the work_ that your product exists to support.

Here are techniques that help.

### The "Scream Test"

The simplest heuristic: **If your product disappeared tomorrow, who would scream first?** Not who would be inconvenienced. Not who would notice in a quarterly review. Who would be unable to do their work _that day_?

For a CRM like Salesforce, the VP of Sales would be frustrated, but it's the account executives who couldn't do their jobs. For a learning management system in a school district, the superintendent signed the contract, but it's the teachers who would have no way to deliver lessons. The person who screams first is almost always your job executor.

### Trace the workflow backward from the outcome

Another approach is to start with the outcome your product ultimately enables and trace backward. Who is the last person to touch the process before the outcome is achieved?

Consider enterprise procurement software. The outcome is "goods and services acquired at the best value while maintaining compliance." Trace backward: who issues the purchase order? Who evaluates vendors? Who identifies the need? In most organizations, procurement managers or buyers are performing these steps. Executives approve budgets, IT maintains the system, but the procurement manager is the one executing the job. That's your executor.

This technique is especially useful in B2B contexts where job titles are misleading. Someone called a "project coordinator" might be executing the core job while the "project manager" is really functioning as a purchase decision maker or an escalation point.

### Observe, don't just ask

If you ask a room of stakeholders "who uses this product to get the job done?", everyone will raise their hand. Executives believe they're the executor because they make decisions that depend on the product's output. IT believes they're the executor because they interact with the product constantly (maintaining it). The actual executor often has the least political power in the room.

Whenever possible, observe the work in context. Watch who opens the product first thing in the morning. Watch whose workflow breaks when the product is slow or down. Watch who has developed workarounds for the product's shortcomings or workarounds are a signal of deep engagement with the core job, not just peripheral interaction.

> **Author's Note:** In my experience, one of the best ways to identify the true executor is to look for who has built workarounds. Spreadsheets, sticky notes, Slack channels that exist solely to compensate for a product's shortcomings. These are almost always maintained by the person actually doing the job. The person who built the shadow spreadsheet to track what the "official" tool doesn't? That's your executor.

## When the executor isn't obvious

The surgeon example is easy. Many real-world products don't have such a clear executor. Here are patterns where teams consistently get it wrong.

### The beneficiary vs. the operator

Sometimes the person who benefits from the job being done and the person who operates the product are different. A parent setting up a child's educational app is operating the product, but is the child the job executor? Or is the parent?

It depends on what you define as the core job. If the job is "learn to read," the child is the executor and the parent is support. If the job is "ensure my child develops age-appropriate literacy skills," the parent is the executor and the app is one of many tools they might use. These are two different markets that would produce different outcome statements and different product decisions.

The same pattern appears in eldercare technology. A monitoring device might be operated by an elderly parent, but if the core job is "ensure the safety of an aging family member," the adult child making the purchasing and configuration decisions might be the true executor. The elderly parent might not even want the product.

There is no universal rule here. The key is to recognize the ambiguity, make a deliberate choice, and document your reasoning.

### Automation and the disappearing executor

A newer challenge: what happens when the job executor is being _replaced_ by the product itself?

Consider automated customer support chatbots. Before the chatbot, a customer service representative executed the job of "resolve customer issues." The chatbot now handles a portion of that job. So who is the job executor? The customer who interacts with the chatbot, the support manager who configures it, or the CS representative who handles escalations?

Again, it depends on framing. If you're building the chatbot product, the support manager configuring it might be your primary executor since their job is "ensure customer issues are resolved efficiently," and the chatbot is a tool they use. But the end customer interacting with the chatbot has their own job ("get my problem resolved"), and if the chatbot fails their needs, the support manager's job also fails.

In automation contexts, I recommend identifying the person who is **accountable for the outcome** as the primary executor, even if they're not the one interacting with the product moment-to-moment. They're the one who will evaluate whether the product is doing its job, and their desired outcomes are what should drive your research.

### The everyone-is-the-executor problem in B2B

In enterprise software, you'll frequently encounter situations where multiple roles all claim to be the executor. A project management tool like Asana might have:

- Individual contributors using it to track their tasks
- Project managers using it to coordinate work across people
- Executives using it to monitor portfolio progress
- Operations teams using it to manage cross-departmental processes

All of these people "use the product." But they're executing different jobs. The IC's job might be "manage my personal workload." The PM's job might be "coordinate cross-functional work to deliver an outcome on time." The executive's job might be "allocate resources across competing priorities."

These aren't just different personas, they're different _markets_ under the ODI formula, each with different outcome statements and different unmet needs. This is a critical distinction: ODI doesn't say "identify one executor and ignore everyone else." It says each executor-job pair is a separate market that can be researched independently. The strategic question is which market to prioritize.

## Who to prioritize?

While identifying job executors and purchase decision makers is necessary, determining which group to target primarily presents a challenge for many teams.

### Platform businesses: two markets, one choice

Platform businesses face what's known as the "chicken and egg problem": the need to build both sides of a marketplace simultaneously to create value. However, successful platforms typically solve this by identifying which side is more crucial for building initial marketplace liquidity.

**Important Note for JTBD:** When dealing with a platform like Uber or Airbnb, you are not researching "one market." You are researching **two distinct markets** that happen to interact.

1. **Market A:** Drivers (Executor) + Earn Income (Job)
2. **Market B:** Passengers (Executor) + Get to Destination (Job)

You must make a strategic choice on which market to prioritize first. Andrew Chen describes this as the **hard side** of the network in his book _The Cold Start Problem_ (2021)[25]. Chen observes that there is typically a minority of users who create disproportionate value but are harder to acquire and retain. Wikipedia illustrates this: hundreds of millions use the platform, but only a tiny fraction actively contribute content.

### Non-platform businesses: follow the frequency and the pain

For non-platform products, the targeting decision typically revolves around three key factors: pain point intensity, purchase decision influence, and accessibility. Modern SaaS tools like Figma illustrate this well. While both individual designers and IT teams are important stakeholders, Figma targeted individual designers first because they had the strongest pain point, could influence purchase decisions through bottom-up adoption, and were easily accessible through design communities.

For the majority of businesses, my recommendation is to focus initial targeting efforts on those who use your product **most frequently**. Which typically are the job executor or end consumer. This approach recognizes that sustainable product adoption and growth usually stem from solving real problems for actual users rather than just appeasing purchase decision makers.

### When you have multiple executors: which one first?

For products like the Asana example above, where multiple executors use the same product for different jobs, you need a tiebreaker. Here's the heuristic I use:

**Start with the executor whose job, if done poorly, would render the product irrelevant to everyone else.**

In the Asana case, if ICs can't manage their personal workload effectively in the tool, project managers have nothing to coordinate, and executives have no data to monitor. The IC is the foundation. Their job comes first.

This doesn't mean you ignore other executors forever. It means your _first_ round of research like your first set of outcome statements and your first survey targets the foundational executor. Once you've built a product that satisfies their core outcomes, you can run a second round for the next executor.

> **Author's Note:** I want to be realistic about this. Researching multiple executor-job pairs sequentially is the ideal. Most teams don't have the time or budget for it. If you can only do one round, pick the executor closest to the core value your product creates and accept that you'll have blind spots for the others. One well-researched market is infinitely more useful than three poorly researched ones.

## What happens when you get the executor wrong

To illustrate why this matters, consider what happens when a team skips this step or gets it wrong.

A healthcare IT company built a clinical documentation platform. The purchasing committee hospital CFOs and CIOs wanted faster billing cycles and regulatory compliance. So the product team optimized for those outcomes: billing code integration, audit trails, compliance dashboards. The platform was a hit in sales demos.

But the actual job executors like the physicians who had to document patient encounters found the product added 15 minutes to every patient visit. The documentation workflow was built around the buyer's compliance needs, not the physician's clinical documentation needs. Within 18 months, physicians had developed workarounds (dictating notes into a separate system and having staff manually re-enter them), utilization plummeted, and the hospital began evaluating replacements.

The product team had researched the wrong market. They'd defined it as "Hospital administrators + ensure regulatory compliance in clinical documentation" when the foundational market was "Physicians + document patient encounters accurately and efficiently." Everything downstream — their outcome statements, their feature priorities, their development sprints was oriented toward the wrong executor's needs.

This is the cost of getting the first half of the formula wrong. The product might sell, but it won't survive.

## Questions to help identify the Job Executor, Product Support Team, and Purchase Decision Maker

### Identifying the Job Executor

The most reliable way to identify your true job executor is to ask **targeted questions** that reveal who actually performs the core functional job:

- Who physically interacts with and operates the product/service on a daily basis?
- Whose success metrics are directly tied to the product's core functionality?
- If the product stopped working, who would be immediately impacted in their ability to complete their work? (The Scream Test)
- In a typical workday, who spends the most time directly using the product?
- Who has built workarounds for the product's limitations?
- Who is the last person to touch the workflow before the outcome is achieved?

### Mapping the Product Lifecycle Support Team

**Support teams** can be identified by examining who handles the peripheral but essential tasks surrounding your product:

- Who handles product setup, configuration, and maintenance?
- When something goes wrong, who gets called first?
- Who manages user access, permissions, and system administration?

### Understanding Purchase Decision Makers

To identify **purchase decision makers**, focus on financial authority and accountability:

- Who owns the budget that pays for this solution?
- Who evaluates competing solutions and creates vendor shortlists?
- Who is accountable for ROI on this purchase?

### Common Pitfalls in Role Identification

Teams frequently encounter several challenges when identifying these roles:

1. **Overemphasizing organizational authority** instead of actual job execution.
2. **Mistaking frequent product interaction** for job execution. A customer support agent may interact with your CRM all day, but the job executor for "manage the customer relationship" might be the account manager who logs in twice a week.
3. **Assuming job executors are always lower-level employees.**
4. **Confusing the beneficiary with the operator.** The person who benefits from the outcome and the person who operates the tool may be different people.
5. **Missing secondary job executors** in platform businesses or in products that serve multiple jobs.

By working through these questions and conducting careful observation, teams can build a clear picture of their key stakeholders and ensure their product development efforts are properly targeted.

## Next Step: Defining the job itself

At this point, you've mapped the stakeholder landscape for your market. You know the difference between the job executor (the person actually doing the work), the product lifecycle support team (the people who install, maintain, and troubleshoot), and the purchase decision maker (the person who controls the budget). You understand that in platform businesses, you're dealing with two distinct markets, each with its own executor and its own job. You know how to handle the tricky cases.

You've also learned who to prioritize: for core product innovation, the job executor almost always takes precedence. Building for buyers instead of users creates shelfware. Building for the hard side of a network creates the foundation for marketplace liquidity.

But knowing _who_ to focus on is only half the equation. The next question is: what are they actually trying to accomplish?

The job executor you've identified doesn't just "use your product." They're trying to achieve something specific. Such as something that exists independent of your solution. A surgeon using surgical instruments isn't just "operating equipment." They're trying to perform precise surgical procedures with minimal patient trauma. A marketing project manager using Asana isn't just "managing tasks." They might be trying to align stakeholders on project progress or translate project goals into an actionable plan.

Defining this core functional job at the right level of abstraction, with the right scope — is the focus of the next chapter. It is arguably the hardest step in the entire ODI process, and the stakeholder identification work you've done here ensures you're defining the right job for the right person.

## Chapter 3 Key Takeaways

- **The Market Formula:** In ODI, a market is defined as `Market = [Job Executor] + [The Job-to-be-Done]`. This chapter focused on getting the first half right.
- **Define your market by the job, not the product:** This reveals the true competitive landscape (e.g., Netflix competes with sleep).
- **Distinguish the roles:** Clearly separate the Job Executor (user), the Product Lifecycle Support Team (installer/maintainer), and the Purchase Decision Maker (buyer).
- **Use practical techniques to find the real executor:** The Scream Test, tracing the workflow backward, observing who builds workarounds, and watching who breaks when the product goes down.
- **Beware the Buyer Trap:** For core product innovation, always prioritize the needs of the Job Executor to avoid building "shelfware."
- **The executor isn't always obvious.** Watch for beneficiary-vs-operator splits, automation replacing executors, and multiple executors using the same product for different jobs.
- **Same product, different jobs:** Even non-platform products may serve multiple job executors with fundamentally different jobs. Each executor-job pair is a separate market. Prioritize the one whose job, if unsatisfied, makes the product irrelevant to everyone else.
- **Two-Sided Markets:** In platform businesses, treat the supply side and demand side as two distinct markets with two different jobs.
- **Getting the executor wrong is expensive.** Every downstream phase — outcome statements, surveys, segments, strategy — inherits whatever you put into the formula. Research the wrong executor and you'll build the wrong product.
